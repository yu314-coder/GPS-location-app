\title{\vspace{-1.5em}\textbf{Recording a route without satellites}\\[0.3em]
\large How accurate a phone's own sensors are, measured against GPS}
\author{Yu Yao-Hsing\\\small\texttt{euler.yu@gmail.com}}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}\noindent
When GPS is lost --- in a tunnel, an underground car park, or an aircraft --- a phone can still
draw the route it travels. It reads \emph{speed} from how the vehicle shakes and \emph{direction}
from its gyroscope and compass, and it never uses GPS after the first point. This paper measures
the newest version of that system on a motorcycle, in a car, on foot and on a plane, by running it
over every earlier recording and comparing the result with GPS recorded at the same time, used only
as the answer key. Direction works in all four: it was within
$30^\circ$ of GPS $73\%$ of the time on a motorcycle, $84\%$ in a car, $93\%$ on foot and $99\%$ on
a flight. Over $74$ journeys ($20$~hours), distance came out $10\%$ short on a motorcycle and within
$1\%$ in a car. Walking came
out $4\%$ long. In the air the app measures the takeoff and holds the speed it reached, which
counted $61\%$ of a flight's distance. On a motorcycle, fast riding reads
slow and slow riding reads fast. A phone with few examples of its own uses a small built-in network
instead; on recordings it had never seen, it was as accurate as a phone with a full store.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the system does}

A route is a series of short steps. Each step needs a length and a direction. With GPS both come
from satellites; here both come from the phone's motion sensors.

\paragraph{Speed from vibration.} A moving vehicle shakes, and it shakes differently at different
speeds~\cite{carspeednet}. (Adding up acceleration instead does not work on a phone: its small
sensor errors grow into kilometres within minutes~\cite{memserrors}.) Every second, the phone takes the last five seconds of up-and-down acceleration, measured
$50$ times a second, and turns it into a short ``fingerprint'' of eleven numbers --- how much
energy the shaking has at each of nine frequency ranges, plus two measures of its overall
strength. Whenever GPS is available, the phone stores fingerprints together with the speed GPS
measured at that moment; it keeps up to $4000$ of them. Without GPS, it looks up the $12$ stored
fingerprints closest to the current one and averages their speeds:
\begin{equation}
\hat v = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12} v_j / d_j^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{12} 1 / d_j^2}
\end{equation}
where $v_j$ is a stored speed and $d_j$ how far its fingerprint is from the current one. If even
the closest stored fingerprint is far away, the phone does not guess. Averaging tends to pull every
answer toward the middle, so a correction learned from the stored examples stretches the answers
back out.

\paragraph{A starting model.} The stored examples come from the phone's own trips, so a new phone
has none. Until it has $3000$, it uses a small network instead:
the same eleven-number fingerprint goes in, a speed comes out. The network was trained on recorded
trips and ships with the app, so it needs nothing from the current trip. Like the store, it declines
a fingerprint unlike anything it was trained on.

\paragraph{Speed in the air.} A smooth cabin reads as standing still, so in the air the app measures
the takeoff instead. From the last moment the phone was still, it follows the phone's turning with
the gyroscope and adds up the push the accelerometer feels, for up to two minutes; then it holds the
speed it reached.

\paragraph{Direction from turns.} The gyroscope measures every turn precisely but does not know
north; the compass knows north but measures where the \emph{phone} points, not where the vehicle
is going --- a known problem for phones carried in different ways~\cite{pdrheading}. In a pocket those differ by some angle $\beta$, which depends on how the phone happens to
sit. The phone learns $\beta$ from the vehicle's own turns. In a turn the vehicle is pushed toward
the inside of the curve, so the sideways push the phone feels, compared with which way the
gyroscope says it is turning, shows where ``forward'' is. Written as complex numbers,
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta = \arg\left(\frac{\sum_{R} z_t}{\sum_{R} |s_t|}+\frac{\sum_{L} z_t}{\sum_{L} |s_t|}\right),
\qquad z_t=\frac{a_t}{|a_t|}\,\overline{i\,s_t},\quad s_t=\operatorname{sgn}(\omega_t)\sqrt{v_t|\omega_t|}
\end{equation}
where $a_t$ is the sideways and forward acceleration measured relative to the compass, $v_t$ the
speed, $\omega_t$ the turn rate, and $R$ and $L$ the seconds turning right and left. The square
root lets the many ordinary bends count beside the few sharp corners. Braking into a
corner and pulling away out of it lean the answer one way in right turns and the other way in left
ones, so the two kinds are averaged apart. Only seconds above $14$~km/h count, and only when the
turn explains at least a tenth of the push: otherwise the push is braking or throttle, and a
reversing car's points backwards. The phone makes a first estimate after
$30$~seconds of riding and keeps improving it; when it improves, the part of the ride already
drawn is redrawn with the better value.

Walking is handled differently: over four seconds, the back-and-forth of the steps lines up with
the direction of walking, and each push-off is sharper than each landing, which says which end of
that line is forward.

\paragraph{The compass reading itself.} The phone's built-in heading is unreliable in one common
pocket position: when the phone sits head-down at an angle, it scattered by about $49^\circ$ a
minute even though the phone was not moving in the pocket. So the system computes its own heading
from the phone's orientation, using whichever edge of the phone is closest to level. That heading
scattered by about $1^\circ$ a minute in the same position.

In a car the heading also drifts with the gyroscope's own small bias: $42^\circ$ in $25$~minutes
on one drive, the phone fixed. A stopped car does not turn, so the phone measures the heading's
turning while stopped and still, as a rate (at most $3^\circ$ a minute), and takes it off. Core
Motion also sets north once, often wrongly in a car, so while the field looks like Earth's
($25$--$65~\mu$T, steady for a minute) the phone corrects that start from the magnetometer.

\paragraph{Only the first point uses GPS.} After that, each step is laid down from the previous
point, along the current direction, for the distance the speed covers in that second.

\section{How it was tested}

GPS was recorded alongside every test and never used by the system; it serves only as the answer
key. So that every result describes the newest version, the newest method was run again over all
earlier recordings, which were made with the phone's raw sensor readings saved --- its
acceleration, rotation and orientation angles, fifty times a second:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Speed.} The app's speed logic was run again on the recorded acceleration: the same
fingerprint, the same $12$-neighbour average, the same refusals and correction, and the same rules
for a stopped vehicle, for a phone being handled, and for holding the last answer. Its memory holds
$4000$ examples drawn at random from every \emph{other} journey, so that no journey was ever
measured with its own GPS in the memory. On twelve recent journeys the re-run came out typically
$4\%$ shorter than what the app itself had drawn.
\item \textbf{Direction.} The direction method, including the drift correction and the
redrawing, was run again on the recorded rotation, orientation and acceleration, with the re-run
speed.
\item \textbf{Starting network.} Each recording was measured by a network trained without it (the
recordings were split into eight groups), and the store was tested holding only $60$ to $4000$
examples.
\item \textbf{Walking.} The app's step detector was run again on the recorded acceleration, with the
stride the phone had learned. The phone's own step counter, which the app also consults, was not
recorded; its share is taken from each journey's first walk, where it added about $2\%$ on
average.
\end{itemize}

Three inputs are taken from the recordings as they were: whether each second was walking or riding
(which older versions decided partly with GPS help the current one no longer uses), Apple's motion
classifier, and the car-park ramp flag. Cabin pressure was not recorded, so on the flight
the moment the app would call it airborne was taken from the same integration's climb rate.

Every result is split by what was moving. A motorcycle and a car were told apart from the sensors
themselves: in a turn a motorcycle leans and the phone leans with it, while a car stays level and
the phone is pushed sideways. This agreed with every journey whose vehicle was known. Walking is the
time the app was counting steps. That gives $39$ motorcycle journeys ($11$~hours, the phone in a
trouser pocket) and $35$ car journeys ($8.6$~hours, the phone in a pocket or lying flat) for speed
and distance, $68$ routes, $65$ recordings for direction, $45$ straight half-minutes and seven longer
walks on foot, and one flight. Four car journeys went into, started in or ended in underground car parks,
where GPS kept reporting positions it did not have; those stretches, found from the barometer, are
left out (Section~\ref{sec:wrong}). Half-minutes that mix walking and riding, or in which the app counted
steps while GPS showed riding speed, are left out.

In a pocket GPS is itself only accurate to $10$--$40$~m, so it was used over stretches long
enough for that not to matter:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Speed and distance} are compared over half-minute stretches between GPS fixes that
are better than $40$~m: the straight distance GPS moved, against the distance the system added.
Walking moves so little in half a minute that only straight stretches between fixes better than
$25$~m are used.
\item \textbf{Direction} is compared every second: the bearing from the GPS position $15$~s
earlier to the one $15$~s later, against the system's heading over the same half-minute. For the
plane, which covers far more ground, the stretch is ten seconds.
\item \textbf{Whole routes} are compared by finding the rotation and the size change that best lay
the drawn route over the GPS track, pinned at the shared starting point. The rotation is how far
the whole route is turned; the size change is how much too big or too small it is.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

Every graph is split by what was moving: a motorcycle, a car, a person walking, or a plane.

\subsection{Speed}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figures/fig1_speed.pdf}
\caption{Speed from the app against speed from GPS. (a,\,b)~Each dot is a half-minute of riding;
the solid line is the middle of each $10$~km/h band and the shading holds the middle half. On the
dashed line the two agree. (c)~Straight half-minutes of walking; triangles are the first minutes
after a ride. (d)~One flight, over time.}
\label{fig:speed}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($818$ half-minutes). The app squeezes the range: $10$--$20$~km/h reads
about $19$, $20$--$40$~km/h is close, and above $60$~km/h it reads about $41$ where GPS measured
$66$. The squeeze comes from averaging twelve examples: when a fingerprint could belong to several
speeds, the average lands in the middle. The average error is $9$~km/h.
\item \textbf{Car} ($733$ half-minutes). Much closer: from $10$ to $60$~km/h the middle reading is
within about $2$~km/h of GPS. Above that it reads low ($62$ where GPS measured $69$, and $71$ where
it measured $87$). The average error is $7.3$~km/h.
\item \textbf{Walking} ($45$ straight half-minutes). About $5$~km/h where GPS measured about
$4.6$, just after a ride as at any other time.
\item \textbf{Plane} (one flight). While taxiing the app reads about $17$~km/h
where GPS measured $18$. In the air it measures the takeoff --- $265$~km/h forty seconds into the
roll, where GPS measured $266$ --- and holds the $317$~km/h it reached; it does not see the aircraft
keep speeding up to $680$~km/h in the climb.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Distance}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figures/fig2_distance.pdf}
\caption{Distance by the app against GPS. (a,\,b)~Each dot is one journey with at least $1$~km that
GPS could check; the shading is within $20\%$. (c)~Straight stretches of walking, added up.
(d)~Distance so far on one flight.}
\label{fig:distance}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($39$ journeys, $176$~km checked). The app added $158$~km, $10\%$ short:
the distance lost at high speed outweighs what is gained at low speed. Of the $38$ journeys with at
least $1$~km checked, the middle one was $5\%$ short and $79\%$ were within $20\%$; the worst were
$65\%$ short and $64\%$ long.
\item \textbf{Car} ($35$ journeys, $163$~km checked). The app added $163$~km, $0.3\%$ short. Of
the $31$ journeys with at least $1$~km checked, the middle one was $1.6\%$ short and $65\%$ were
within $20\%$; the worst were $37\%$ short and $67\%$ long.
\item \textbf{Walking}. Over straight stretches the app was $4\%$ long: $7\%$ long in the first
minutes after a ride and $7\%$ short at other times.
\item \textbf{Plane}. The app counted $45$~km of the $74$~km flown ($61\%$).
\end{itemize}

Motorcycle and car together, the app added $321$~km where GPS measured $340$~km, $5.5\%$ short.

\subsection{Direction}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{figures/fig3_direction.pdf}
\caption{How often the direction is within a given angle of GPS, compared every second with a
half-minute of GPS track (ten seconds for the plane).}
\label{fig:direction}
\end{figure}

Direction holds up in every case (Figure~\ref{fig:direction}):

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($40$ recordings): within $30^\circ$ $73\%$ of the time; middle error
$16^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Car} ($25$ recordings): within $30^\circ$ $84\%$ of the time; middle error $14^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Walking} (seven walks with the phone in a pocket): within $30^\circ$ $93\%$ of the
time; middle error $10^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Plane} (one flight): within $30^\circ$ $99\%$ of the time; middle error $6^\circ$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The flight, second by second}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figures/fig5_flight.pdf}
\caption{One flight, from the gate to the climb. (a)~The direction of travel from the GPS track
(black) and from this method, which uses no GPS (purple). (b)~The aircraft's speed from GPS, to show
where it was taxiing, taking off and climbing.}
\label{fig:flight}

Figure~\ref{fig:flight} follows the flight with the same direction method as on the road. While
taxiing, the middle error was $9^\circ$; in the air it was $4^\circ$. Ten seconds were more than
$30^\circ$ off: the pushback, when the aircraft moves tail first while the method gives the way the

no clear direction. In the climb the error stays small, about $1^\circ$ by the end.

\subsection{A new phone}\label{sec:network}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{figures/fig6_network.pdf}
\caption{The built-in network (blue) next to the main model with a full store (grey), measured on
the same journeys; the network only on recordings it had never seen. (a)~Share of the GPS distance
counted. (b)~Average speed error. (c)~Journeys within $20\%$ of GPS. (d)~A store that is still
filling up, against the network; the dotted line is where the app hands over.}
\label{fig:network}
\end{figure}

A new phone has no examples of its own, so it starts with the built-in network.
Figure~\ref{fig:network} puts it next to the main model with a full store, kind of travel by kind of
travel:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle.} The network misses by $9.1$~km/h on average and the main model by
$9.0$. The network counted $91.5\%$ of the distance and the main model $90.0\%$; $79\%$ of journeys
came within $20\%$ with either.
\item \textbf{Car.} The network misses by $6.7$~km/h and the main model by $7.3$; they counted
$101.7\%$ and $99.7\%$ of the distance, with $74\%$ and $65\%$ of journeys within $20\%$.
\item \textbf{Walking.} The same, $103.7\%$: walking is counted by steps, not by either speed model.
\item \textbf{Plane.} The same, $61\%$: in the air both hand over to the measured takeoff.
\end{itemize}

A store that is still filling does worse (Figure~\ref{fig:network}d): with $60$ examples the average
error was $10.6$~km/h, with $1000$ it was $8.8$, and it draws level with the network ($8.3$) at about
$3000$. That is where the app hands over from the network to the store, which then keeps adapting to
the phone's own vehicle and pocket.

\section{Where it goes wrong}\label{sec:wrong}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{On a motorcycle, fast riding reads slow and slow riding reads fast.} Above
$60$~km/h the speed is under two-thirds of the true value (Figure~\ref{fig:speed}a). Over a whole
journey the two partly cancel, but a journey that is mostly fast comes out short. A car does not
have this problem below $60$~km/h.
\item \textbf{The first minute of a ride.} Until the pocket angle is learned, the direction comes
from the compass alone, which in a pocket can be far off. The redrawing fixes the saved route
afterwards, but a very short ride may never learn the angle well.
\item \textbf{A phone that is picked up.} A hand shakes the phone, and shaking is what the speed
model reads: going down into a car park, a lifted phone read $88$~km/h. A hand can no longer raise
the speed (re-run, that stretch reads about $6$~km/h), but a phone picked up while slowing keeps its
last speed until it is still: once $48$~km/h for ten seconds after stopping. A phone that shifts in
the pocket turns the rest of the ride by about as much as it moved.
\item \textbf{Underground car parks.} GPS cannot check the app there: in four car journeys it kept
claiming $10$~m accuracy underground, its position frozen while parked and wandering on the ramps,
so those stretches are left out. Parked underground for five and a half minutes, the app added
$2$~m. A car crawling up a concrete ramp shakes like one driving at $40$~km/h, so distance is not
counted while climbing and turning together; once that test fired on an ordinary road and lost
about $115$~m.
\item \textbf{A magnet beside the phone.} By a car's MagSafe charger the phone read up to
$2{,}600~\mu$T, fifty times Earth's field, yet called its compass well calibrated. The iPhone's own
Compass then drifted $49^\circ$ with the gyroscope; the app ignores such a field.
\item \textbf{Rides never recognised as driving.} Without GPS, the app knows it is in a vehicle
from Apple's motion classifier or its own speed reading. On one short pocket ride the classifier
never said ``driving'' and the step counter took the engine for footsteps: $59\%$ short.
\item \textbf{Aircraft after the takeoff.} The app holds the speed the takeoff reached, so it misses
the speed-up in the climb: it held $317$~km/h while the aircraft reached $680$, and counted $61\%$ of
the distance (Figure~\ref{fig:speed}d). The phone's own orientation filter takes a takeoff's steady
push for a tilt~\cite{quatcf}, which is why the raw accelerometer is used. Examples learned on
earlier flights with GPS would take over, which one flight could not test.
\end{itemize}

\section{What did not work}

Measured against GPS on the same recordings, none of these did better:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Direction from speeding up and braking.} The push when accelerating in a straight
line points forward, so it should give direction. It was $44^\circ$ off; turns work far better.
\item \textbf{Leaving the speed out of the turns} (weighting by the change in turn rate):
$15$--$40^\circ$ off on one drive, as braking into and out of corners added up.
\item \textbf{Other direction ideas}: the axis the motorcycle leans about ($25^\circ$ off), the
direction of the rider's thigh ($73^\circ$ off), and the swing of the thigh for telling forward
from backward when walking (wrong more often than right).
\item \textbf{Other riding-or-walking tests} (a steady push; speed overruling steps) misfired.
\item \textbf{Following the rest of a flight.} Speed from turns: the phone's own turning
($0.4$--$1^\circ$/s) hid the gentle turns. Integrating the whole climb: one setting only.
\item \textbf{A better starting network}: the last ten seconds as input did not help.
\end{itemize}

\section{Lessons}\label{sec:lessons}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The phone's own definitions caused more error than the models.} Its built-in heading
becomes unreliable in a common pocket position and drifts slowly in a car, and its ``user
acceleration'' points the opposite way to the vehicle's acceleration. Each of these, once found, mattered more than any change to
the estimators.
\item \textbf{GPS can hide in decisions as well as in distance.} The no-GPS mode never drew a metre
from GPS, yet it still used GPS to decide whether the phone was riding or walking, and to set the
first heading. Only reading every place the mode touched GPS found them; both are gone.
\item \textbf{A re-run must do everything the app does,} stops and a phone in the hand included;
with those rules in, it comes within about $4\%$ of what the app drew.
\item \textbf{Check what a counter stores while nothing reads it.} The step detector counted road
bumps as steps all ride and only a walk emptied it, so the next walk came out twice its length.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Limits of this evaluation}

All recordings come from one person and one iPhone, mostly in one city, with only one flight;
other people, phones and vehicles may behave differently, the starting network most of all. Most
results re-run the newest method on earlier recordings rather than coming from live use, and GPS in
a pocket is only accurate to tens of metres, so small errors cannot be measured reliably.
