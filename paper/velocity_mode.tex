\begin{abstract}\noindent
A phone can record where it has been without satellite positioning, by reading speed from how the
vehicle shakes and direction from its motion sensors. We describe the method as shipped in a
consumer iOS workout application, and report its accuracy on twenty-three instrumented journeys by
car, motorcycle and on foot. Each was recorded with GPS running alongside, used only as a
reference: we call that the \emph{real} speed, and what the sensors produced the \emph{estimated}
speed. Driving fast and steadily with the phone resting in the vehicle, estimated distance is
within $3.8\%$ over $15$~km and direction within $5^\circ$; in slow stop-start traffic the same
system reads $20$--$60\%$ long. We show that this gap is a property of the signal rather than a
defect: the estimate reads one to two km/h high at any speed, which is $2\%$ of a motorway pace
and $40\%$ of a crawl. We give equal space to eight approaches that were implemented, measured and
rejected. Three failed for one reason. An accelerometer cannot tell gravity apart from the vehicle
pushing it --- long known in attitude estimation --- and on this platform the sensor fusion that
resolves that ambiguity is the vendor's, cannot be replaced, and quietly removes the very signal
that aircraft dead reckoning, direction-from-acceleration and takeoff detection all rely on. We
report how much that costs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Satellite positioning fails where routes are most often wanted: tunnels, underground car parks,
dense urban canyons, and aircraft cabins. The standard response is inertial dead reckoning ---
integrate acceleration twice --- which is well known to diverge, since accelerometer bias is
indistinguishable from sustained real acceleration and its double integral grows without bound.

An alternative exists for wheeled vehicles. Road and engine vibration scale with speed, so a
spectral signature of vertical acceleration can be mapped to speed directly, without
integration and therefore without accumulation. CarSpeedNet~\cite{carspeednet} reports
RMSE $1.8$~m/s and MAE $0.72$~m/s from $13.2$~hours of accelerometer-only driving, at the same
$4$-second window length we arrive at independently. Its $0.5$-hour test holdout is drawn from
that same collection, so the reported figure describes journeys much like the ones it learned from.

This paper describes such a system built into a shipping application, and --- more usefully ---
reports what happens outside the distribution, on a phone that is sometimes mounted, sometimes
pocketed, sometimes in a hand, in a car, on a motorcycle, and once in an aircraft.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item An out-of-distribution evaluation of vibration-derived speed across carry positions and
vehicle types, on one device and one user, with per-regime error.
\item The measured cost of the specific-force ambiguity on a consumer platform: three distinct
approaches defeated, with magnitudes.
\item Eight negative results reported in full, including two that look correct in aggregate and
fail per-query.
\item Three sensor-platform defects that silently invalidated weeks of data, offered as evidence
that platform semantics dominate modelling error in this setting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Speed from vibration.} The closest work is CarSpeedNet~\cite{carspeednet}, a CNN
taking three-axis smartphone accelerometer input, reporting RMSE $1.8$~m/s and MAE $0.72$~m/s
over $13.2$~hours of driving. Related approaches estimate speed from chassis
vibration with CNNs~\cite{cnnspeed}, infer position directly from vehicle vibration with an
IMU~\cite{vibposition}, and use learned momentary speed to constrain strapdown inertial
navigation~\cite{constrainsins}. Outside the inertial domain, vehicle speed has been estimated
from tyre--road noise, where band energies at $1$--$4$~kHz give $1.4\%$ average
error~\cite{soundspeed} and the underlying physics --- tread-block passage at
$f = NV/L$ --- gives a direct speed dependence.

Our estimator is deliberately simpler than these: a $k$-NN regressor over log-spectral band
energies, chosen because it can \emph{decline} to answer when nothing resembling the query has
been observed, which a trained network cannot. The contribution here is not the estimator but
the evaluation regime.

\paragraph{Published accuracy is usually measured on familiar data.} CarSpeedNet's $0.5$-hour holdout comes
from the same $13.2$-hour collection as its training data. This matters: we measure $+1.0$ to $+1.7$~km/h bias on
a store built from matched journeys and $+6.8$~km/h on the same device once the store contains
other carry positions and vehicles --- a degradation invisible to a random split. The
transportation-mode literature has confronted this more directly; the Sussex--Huawei Locomotion
dataset~\cite{shl} was assembled specifically to enable reproducible cross-user evaluation,
with benchmark F1 near $0.93$ for eight-class mode recognition~\cite{shlbench}. No equivalent
public dataset exists for \emph{speed} regression across carry positions.

\paragraph{Inertial dead reckoning.} That double integration of consumer MEMS output is
unusable is well established: laboratory characterisation of smartphone IMUs reports position
error exceeding $1$~km within $100$~s from pure inertial
propagation~\cite{memserrors}, with error growth dominated by uncompensated bias and varying
substantially between handsets. Learned approaches to inertial odometry~\cite{solin} and the
broader survey of deep inertial positioning~\cite{dlsurvey} attack this by replacing integration
with regression, as we do. Standard mitigations --- zero-velocity updates~\cite{zupt} and map
matching~\cite{mapmatch} --- bound rather than remove the drift.

\paragraph{Heading and carry position.} Pedestrian dead reckoning divides into step detection,
heading estimation and position propagation~\cite{pdr3d}, and heading is consistently the
limiting term. Carry position is known to dominate: measured heading standard deviations of
$6.22^\circ$, $6.82^\circ$ and $14.68^\circ$ for holding, calling and swinging respectively,
with corresponding final position errors of $2.11$, $2.34$ and $4.5$~m~\cite{pdrheading}. Our
$\beta$ term is the vehicular analogue of the carry-mode misalignment that literature addresses
for pedestrians, and our finding that it cannot be recovered without an external reference is
consistent with theirs.

\paragraph{The specific-force ambiguity.} An accelerometer measures specific force and cannot
separate gravity from sustained linear acceleration. This is standard, and attitude-estimation
work explicitly compensates for it, either by estimating disturbance acceleration
adaptively~\cite{disturbaccel} or by weighting the accelerometer correction by how far its
magnitude departs from $g$~\cite{quatcf}. What we add is the downstream consequence on a
platform where the fusion is not ours to modify: the vendor-supplied attitude and
gravity-removed acceleration are the only interface, and their handling of sustained
acceleration silently defeats three otherwise reasonable designs (\S5.2).

\section{Method}

\subsection{Signature}

Vertical acceleration $x[n]$ is sampled at $50$~Hz into a ring buffer of $N = 256$ samples
($4.0$~s), windowed and transformed:
\begin{align}
w[n] &= \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)\\
X[k] &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\big(x[n]-\bar{x}\big)\,w[n]\,e^{-2\pi i k n / N}
\end{align}
Energy is accumulated into nine logarithmically spaced bands bounded at
$0.4$, $0.8$, $1.6$, $2.5$, $4$, $6$, $9$, $13$, $18$ and $24$~Hz:
\begin{equation}
f_b = \ln\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k \in b} |X[k]|^2 + \epsilon\Big), \quad b = 1\ldots 9
\end{equation}
Two time-domain terms complete an eleven-dimensional signature:
\begin{equation}
f_{10} = \ln \sigma(x), \qquad
f_{11} = \ln\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{N-1}\textstyle\sum_n |x[n]-x[n-1]|\Big)
\end{equation}

The window length was measured rather than assumed. At $4$~s the held-out bias is
$+1.0$~km/h at $2$--$10$~km/h; doubling to $8$~s raises it to $+6.0$, because the window
then straddles changes in speed. Finer low-frequency banding changed nothing.

\subsection{Speed}

No functional form is imposed. The system stores $(\mathbf{f}, v)$ pairs whenever GPS supplies a
speed, and answers by locality. With running feature means $\mu_i$ and variances $\sigma_i^2$,
\begin{align}
d^2(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{11}\left(\frac{f_i-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}-\frac{g_i-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^{\!2}\\
\hat{v} &= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K} w_j v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} w_j},
\qquad w_j = \frac{1}{d_j^2 + \epsilon}
\end{align}
with $K = 12$. Crucially the estimator is allowed to decline:
\begin{equation}
\min_j d_j^2 > 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{no answer}
\end{equation}
Error scales directly with match distance --- MAE $5.3$~km/h below $d^2\!=\!1.03$, rising to
$16.9$ in the range $2.12$--$4.36$ --- so refusing a distant match removes the worst readings.

\subsection{Regime separation}

A store holding several vehicles holds several mutually incompatible mappings. Measured
directly: a ground-trained model reported $19$~km/h at a $900$~km/h cruise, because cruise is
\emph{quieter} than taxiing and the slowest stored signature was the closest match. After flight
data entered the same store, a city drive reported a mean of $337$~km/h against a true $30$.
Air and ground are therefore stored and searched as separate partitions.

\subsection{Direction}

Turn angle comes from the gyroscope, absolute direction from the magnetometer:
\begin{equation}
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} + \Delta\psi_{\text{gyro}}
+ \kappa\big(\psi_{\text{datum}} + \beta - \theta_{t-1}\big)
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the \emph{carry offset}: the angle between where the phone points and where the
body travels. $\beta$ is not observable without an external reference, so it is learned from GPS
course over the first $180$~s of a workout and then frozen --- which mirrors the physical case
the mode exists for: signal on the way to the aircraft, none thereafter.

\subsection{Projection}

\begin{equation}
\Delta s = \hat{v}\,\Delta t, \quad
\phi_t = \phi_{t-1} + \frac{\Delta s \cos\theta_t}{R}, \quad
\lambda_t = \lambda_{t-1} + \frac{\Delta s \sin\theta_t}{R\cos\phi_t}
\end{equation}
Only the first point of a route originates from GPS.

\section{Results}

Throughout this section \emph{real} means the speed or distance GPS recorded at the same instant
as the estimate, and \emph{estimated} means what the app produced from the motion sensors alone.
Real distance is the integral of GPS speed over time, not the sum of fix-to-fix jumps: on one
$15$~km journey those two differ by $35\%$ ($14{,}445$~m against $19{,}496$~m), because position
scatter adds length that was never travelled.

\subsection{Why slow journeys read long}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_bias.pdf}
\caption{How much the estimate reads above the real speed, by real speed. Tested by hiding each
stored example in turn and asking the model to recover it.}
\label{fig:bias}
\end{figure}

The ordering in Table~\ref{tab:journeys} follows average speed, not any property of the route, and
Figure~\ref{fig:bias} explains why. Tested against $4{,}178$ stored examples from matched journeys
--- hiding each one in turn and asking the model to recover it --- the estimate reads
$1.0$ to $1.7$~km/h high at \emph{every} speed.

A fixed error in km/h is not a fixed error in percent. Two km/h on a $50$~km/h road is $2\%$; the
same two km/h on a $5$~km/h crawl is $40\%$. So the estimator that looks excellent on a motorway
looks poor in traffic, without anything about it having changed.

This is also why no correction helps. Subtracting a constant $6$~km/h --- fitted on two journeys
where the estimate happened to read exactly that much high --- repaired those two and made three
others worse, reducing total absolute error across all journeys by only $7\%$.

\subsection{Direction: the angle the phone sits at}

The phone rarely points the way the vehicle travels. It sits at some angle in a cradle, a pocket
or a cup holder, and that angle --- $\beta$ in \S3.4 --- has to be subtracted before the recorded
heading means anything. It cannot be worked out from the motion sensors alone (\S5.2), so it is
measured from GPS during the first three minutes of a journey and then held fixed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_heading.pdf}
\caption{The same journeys with the angle unlearned and learned. Left: how far the whole route is

$26^\circ$, which put the finish $4{,}174$~m from where it actually was. Re-running the same

The two worst journeys in Table~\ref{tab:journeys} are both car parks, and

Detecting a ramp needs two signals together. The barometer alone flags every hill --- $8$~km of
one $15$~km journey, where the estimate is fine. Rotation alone flags every junction. A helical
ramp is the only thing that climbs steadily \emph{and} turns continuously the same way, so the app
withholds distance when the barometer shows more than $0.08$~m/s of sustained climb and the
gyroscope more than $150^\circ$ of same-direction turning within $30$~s.

We report these in full because each cost real effort and three share one cause.

A flight was recorded with GPS valid on all $1323$ ticks, reaching $706$~km/h. Replaying it with
GPS removed (Figure~\ref{fig:flight}, Table~\ref{tab:flight}) shows the problem clearly: signal is

The correction that should rescue this --- adding up the acceleration along the direction of
travel --- recovers about $60$~km/h of a $679$~km/h climb. After $400$~seconds of held speed it had
contributed $-2$~km/h. Direction, by contrast, is unaffected: $3.9^\circ$ median across the same
flight. The route points the right way and is the wrong length.

\subsection{One cause, three failures}\label{sec:onecause}

\textbf{Core Motion's attitude filter reinterprets sustained linear acceleration as a change in
the gravity direction.} The ambiguity itself is standard~\cite{disturbaccel,quatcf}; what is
reported here is its measured cost when the fusion is vendor-supplied and cannot be replaced. A takeoff roll is the most sustained acceleration a phone will ever
experience, and is therefore precisely what the filter removes. This defeated:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \emph{Aircraft speed integration}, above.
\item \emph{Deriving $\beta$ from acceleration.} A vehicle accelerates along its axis, so the
world-frame acceleration bearing should give the travel direction. Measured against known
answers on three drives, circular concentration was $R = 0.15$, $0.18$, $0.19$ --- noise.
\item \emph{Inertial takeoff detection.} Integrating positive along-track acceleration and
latching at $45$~m/s in one unbroken episode should identify a takeoff and nothing else.
Replayed: today's car $52.5$~m/s (latched), a second car $209.9$ (latched), a motorcycle $328.5$
(latched), a two-minute \emph{walk} $119.7$ (latched), and the actual flight $15.4$ --- never
latched. Backwards on every case. The episode rarely breaks in a vibrating vehicle because road
noise holds the signal above the floor, so the integral accumulates the positive half of the
noise indefinitely.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Regime identification does not transfer}

Whole-session fingerprints --- the mean normalised signature of a workout --- separate regimes
cleanly. Across eight sessions, same-vehicle-and-carry pairs sit $0.54$--$1.70$ apart,
same-vehicle-different-carry $1.81$--$2.87$, and different-vehicle $4.30$--$6.97$.

Restricting the neighbour search by that fingerprint nevertheless made prediction \emph{worse}
($+2.2 \rightarrow +3.4$~km/h). The ranking is unreliable at the individual level: for one car
journey the nearest stored session was a car ($1.38$), but the second was a \emph{hand-held}
session ($1.54$) and the third a \emph{motorcycle in a pocket} ($1.74$), both ahead of the same
car driven the same morning ($2.29$).

\subsection{Confidence signals that do not work}

Neighbour disagreement is the natural confidence measure for a $k$-NN regressor. It fails here
in the wrong direction: out-of-distribution motorcycle queries had a \emph{tighter} neighbour
spread than in-distribution car queries ($5.2$ vs $12.4$~km/h), because they land consistently
in one wrong region. Session-level fingerprint distance correlated with speed bias at
$r = -0.01$ over six journeys.

\subsection{Discriminating a hijacked pedometer}

On a motorcycle, engine vibration is counted as footsteps. Apple's activity classifier reported
\texttt{automotive} on $636$ ticks and \texttt{walking} on $271$ of one continuous ride, and each
flicker allowed the pedometer to take over: it drove $27\%$ of the ride, reporting a mean
$3.3$~km/h while GPS recorded $49.6$.

Three per-tick discriminators were measured against real walking and rejected: vibration energy
($79\%$ caught at $21\%$ false), gait-band spectral concentration (distributions overlap), and
both combined with rotation rate ($69\%$ at $7\%$). What worked was history rather than
instantaneous features: across two real walks the classifier \emph{never} reported automotive in
$190$ stepping ticks, while on motorcycles the median gap since it last did was $84$~s. Requiring
two minutes of silence before a pedometer-driven revocation suppressed $61\%$ of false
hand-overs at zero measured cost.

\section{Systems failures}

Three defects were not algorithmic, and each silently invalidated data for weeks.

\textbf{A stale reading is not a missing reading.} \texttt{CLLocationManager} suspends heading
updates outside the foreground, and the cached property is never cleared. On an $81$-minute drive
the heading datum held \emph{one value for $4790$ of $4832$ seconds} while the device physically
rotated $5264^\circ$. A fallback to Core Motion's heading had been added for the missing case and
was never reached, because it was written as \texttt{clHeading ?? motionHeading} and a frozen
value is not \texttt{nil}.

\textbf{Background permission gating.} \texttt{allowsBackgroundLocationUpdates} was enabled only
under \emph{Always} authorisation. Under \emph{When In Use} the process was suspended
mid-journey: a single $371$-second hole in a $21$-minute drive, during which the $50$~Hz stream
delivered $49$ samples where $18{,}550$ were due, accounting for the entire $20\%$ distance
shortfall.

\textbf{Instrumentation that cannot express the failure.} A $17$-minute workout the user believed
was recording without GPS had the mode active for $34$~seconds; $63$ GPS fixes entered the route.
The guarantee held --- the code path returns early --- but nothing in the log stated the mode's
state, and it took an hour to establish from a side effect. Two columns now record it directly.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Hand or pocket carry destroys the signal.} At matched speeds a hand-held phone
reads $0.6$--$0.8$ lower in the summary feature, and the feature stops varying with speed
altogether: $7.10, 7.08, 7.07, 7.13, 7.25$ across $10$ to $65$~km/h. Reported speed becomes a
constant near $15$--$25$~km/h regardless of truth.
\item \textbf{Stationary vehicles with running engines.} A motorcycle at a red light reads
$5.2$~km/h, accumulating $690$~m over $339$ stationary ticks. No tested signal separates an
idling engine from a slow crawl.
\item \textbf{Car-park ramps.} A vehicle crawling a concrete spiral shakes as hard as one at
$40$~km/h on a road; measured error is $+169\%$ to $+297\%$. Detection requires two signals
jointly --- barometric climb above $0.08$~m/s \emph{and} signed net rotation above $150^\circ$
over $30$~s --- since the barometer alone flags every hill and rotation alone every junction.
\item \textbf{Unseen vehicles.} The model answers only for conditions it has observed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{One device, one user, three vehicles.} All $23$ journeys were recorded on a single
iPhone by one person, in one city, using one car, one motorcycle and walking. Every quantity here
should be read as characterising that configuration. The transportation-mode literature moved to
multi-user public datasets~\cite{shl} precisely because single-subject results do not transfer,
and we have no evidence ours do.

\paragraph{GPS as ground truth.} Errors are measured against GPS recorded simultaneously. Where
GPS is degraded --- underground, and in the urban canyon immediately after --- the reference is
itself uncertain. We use the integral of GPS speed rather than the sum of fix-to-fix
displacements, because the latter accumulates position scatter: on one $15$~km drive the two
differ by $35\%$ ($14{,}445$~m against $19{,}496$~m), and the fix-summed figure is the wrong one.
Segments with no valid GPS speed are excluded and reported separately, but a systematic bias in
GPS speed at low speed would propagate into our low-speed figures undetected.

\paragraph{No baseline comparison.} We do not evaluate a published method on this data, so we
cannot say whether a CNN trained on the same journeys would degrade the same way outside its
distribution. The comparison we do make --- matched store versus mixed store on identical
held-out data --- isolates store composition, not architecture.

\paragraph{Thresholds fitted on few journeys.} The ramp detector ($0.08$~m/s climb with
$150^\circ$ signed rotation over $30$~s) was selected by sweeping against seven journeys and
choosing the lowest total absolute error. That is a small sample, and one journey regressed from
$-7.1\%$ to $-31.7\%$ under the chosen setting because its ramp over-read had been cancelling an
under-read elsewhere. We report it rather than tuning it away.

\paragraph{Data availability.} The logs are per-tick CSV plus $50$~Hz raw traces, and the results
here are reproducible from them. They are not currently public. Releasing them would make this a
labelled multi-vehicle, multi-carry vibration corpus, which to our knowledge does not otherwise
exist; we consider that the most useful next step.

\section{Conclusion}

Vibration-derived speed is a sound basis for GPS-free route recording within its regime: fast,
steady travel with the phone mechanically coupled to the vehicle, where we measure $3.8\%$
distance error over $15$~km and $5^\circ$ of heading error. Outside that regime the failures are
not gradual --- a hand on the phone removes the signal entirely, and an aircraft's defining
acceleration is erased by the platform's own sensor fusion.

We would emphasise two lessons over any result. First, the dominant error sources across this
work were not modelling choices but sensor-platform semantics: a suspended process, a stale
cached heading, an attitude filter making a defensible choice that happens to destroy the signal
of interest. Second, an estimator that reports without expressing doubt is dangerous in a way
that a wrong estimator is not; every failure above persisted for as long as it did because the
system reported it with the same confidence as a correct reading. The shipped application now
exposes its own error bars to the user.
