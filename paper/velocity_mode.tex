\title{\vspace{-1.5em}\textbf{Recording a route without satellites}\\[0.3em]
\large How accurate a phone's own sensors are, measured against GPS}
\author{Yu Yao-Hsing\\\small\texttt{euler.yu@gmail.com}}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}\noindent
When GPS is lost --- in a tunnel, an underground car park, or an aircraft --- a phone can still
draw the route it travels. It reads \emph{speed} from how the vehicle shakes and \emph{direction}
from its gyroscope and compass, and it never uses GPS after the first point. This paper measures
the newest version of that system on a motorcycle, in a car, on foot and on a plane, by running it
over every earlier recording and comparing the result with GPS recorded at the same time, used only
as the answer key. Direction works in all four: it was within $30^\circ$ of GPS $72\%$ of the time
on a motorcycle, $69\%$ in a car, $93\%$ on foot and $99\%$ on a flight. Over $72$ journeys
($19$~hours), distance came out $10\%$ short on a motorcycle and $1\%$ short in a car. Walking came
out $4\%$ long, and in the air speed cannot be measured at all. On a motorcycle, fast riding reads
slow and slow riding reads fast.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the system does}

A route is a series of short steps. Each step needs a length and a direction. With GPS both come
from satellites; here both come from the phone's motion sensors.

\paragraph{Speed from vibration.} A moving vehicle shakes, and it shakes differently at different
speeds~\cite{carspeednet}. (Adding up acceleration instead does not work on a phone: its small
sensor errors grow into kilometres within minutes~\cite{memserrors}.) Every second, the phone takes the last five seconds of up-and-down acceleration, measured
$50$ times a second, and turns it into a short ``fingerprint'' of eleven numbers --- how much
energy the shaking has at each of nine frequency ranges, plus two measures of its overall
strength. Whenever GPS is available, the phone stores fingerprints together with the speed GPS
measured at that moment; it keeps up to $4000$ of them. Without GPS, it looks up the $12$ stored
fingerprints closest to the current one and averages their speeds:
\begin{equation}
\hat v = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12} v_j / d_j^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{12} 1 / d_j^2}
\end{equation}
where $v_j$ is a stored speed and $d_j$ how far its fingerprint is from the current one. If even
the closest stored fingerprint is far away, the phone does not guess. Averaging tends to pull every
answer toward the middle, so a correction learned from the stored examples stretches the answers
back out.

\paragraph{Direction from turns.} The gyroscope measures every turn precisely but does not know
north; the compass knows north but measures where the \emph{phone} points, not where the vehicle
is going --- a known problem for phones carried in different ways~\cite{pdrheading}. In a pocket those differ by some angle $\beta$, which depends on how the phone happens to
sit. The phone learns $\beta$ from the vehicle's own turns. In a turn the vehicle is pushed toward
the inside of the curve, so the sideways push the phone feels, compared with which way the
gyroscope says it is turning, shows where ``forward'' is. Written as complex numbers,
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta = \arg \sum_t \frac{a_t}{|a_t|}\,\overline{i\,v_t\,\omega_t}
\end{equation}
where $a_t$ is the sideways and forward acceleration measured relative to the compass, $v_t$ the
speed and $\omega_t$ the turn rate. The phone makes a first estimate after $30$~seconds of riding
and keeps improving it; when it improves, the part of the ride already drawn is redrawn with the
better value.

Walking is handled differently: over four seconds, the back-and-forth of the steps lines up with
the direction of walking, and each push-off is sharper than each landing, which says which end of
that line is forward.

\paragraph{The compass reading itself.} The phone's built-in heading is unreliable in one common
pocket position: when the phone sits head-down at an angle, it scattered by about $49^\circ$ a
minute even though the phone was not moving in the pocket. So the system computes its own heading
from the phone's orientation, using whichever edge of the phone is closest to level. That heading
scattered by about $1^\circ$ a minute in the same position.

\paragraph{Only the first point uses GPS.} After that, each step is laid down from the previous
point, along the current direction, for the distance the speed covers in that second.

\section{How it was tested}

GPS was recorded alongside every test and never used by the system; it serves only as the answer
key. So that every result describes the newest version, the newest method was run again over all
earlier recordings, which were made with the phone's raw sensor readings saved --- its
acceleration, rotation and orientation angles, fifty times a second:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Speed.} The app's speed logic was run again on the recorded acceleration: the same
fingerprint, the same $12$-neighbour average, the same refusals and correction, and the same rules
for a stopped vehicle, for a phone being handled, and for holding the last answer. Its memory holds
$4000$ examples drawn at random from every \emph{other} journey, so that no journey was ever
measured with its own GPS in the memory. On ten recent journeys the re-run came out typically $5\%$
shorter than what the app itself had drawn.
\item \textbf{Direction.} The direction method, including the redrawing, was run again on the
recorded rotation and orientation, with the re-run speed.
\item \textbf{Walking.} The app's step detector was run again on the recorded acceleration, with the
stride the phone had learned. The phone's own step counter, which the app also consults, was not
recorded; its share is taken from each journey's first walk, where it added about $2\%$ on
average.
\end{itemize}

Three inputs are taken from the recordings as they were: whether each second was walking or riding,
Apple's motion classifier, and the car-park ramp flag. Cabin pressure was not recorded, so on the
flight the app's switch to examples learned on earlier flights could not be re-run.

Every result is split by what was moving. A motorcycle and a car were told apart from the sensors
themselves: in a turn a motorcycle leans and the phone leans with it, while a car stays level and
the phone is pushed sideways. This agreed with every journey whose vehicle was known. Walking is the
time the app was counting steps. That gives $39$ motorcycle journeys ($11$~hours, the phone in a
trouser pocket) and $33$ car journeys ($7.5$~hours, the phone in a pocket or lying flat) for speed
and distance, $66$ routes, $64$ recordings for direction, $43$ straight half-minutes and seven longer
walks on foot, and one flight. Half-minutes that mix walking and riding, or in which the app counted
steps while GPS showed riding speed, are left out.

In a pocket GPS is itself only accurate to $10$--$40$~m, so it was used over stretches long
enough for that not to matter:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Speed and distance} are compared over half-minute stretches between GPS fixes that
are better than $40$~m: the straight distance GPS moved, against the distance the system added.
Walking moves so little in half a minute that only straight stretches between fixes better than
$25$~m are used.
\item \textbf{Direction} is compared every second: the bearing from the GPS position $15$~s
earlier to the one $15$~s later, against the system's heading over the same half-minute. For the
plane, which covers far more ground, the stretch is ten seconds.
\item \textbf{Whole routes} are compared by finding the rotation and the size change that best lay
the drawn route over the GPS track, pinned at the shared starting point. The rotation is how far
the whole route is turned; the size change is how much too big or too small it is.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

Every graph is split by what was moving: a motorcycle, a car, a person walking, or a plane.

\subsection{Speed}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/fig1_speed.pdf}
\caption{Speed from the app against speed from GPS. (a,\,b)~Each dot is a half-minute of riding;
the solid line is the middle of each $10$~km/h band and the shading holds the middle half. On the
dashed line the two agree. (c)~Straight half-minutes of walking; triangles are the first minutes
after a ride. (d)~One flight, over time.}
\label{fig:speed}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($818$ half-minutes). The app squeezes the range: $10$--$20$~km/h reads
about $19$, $20$--$40$~km/h is close, and above $60$~km/h it reads about $41$ where GPS measured
$66$. The squeeze comes from averaging twelve examples: when a fingerprint could belong to several
speeds, the average lands in the middle. The average error is $9$~km/h.
\item \textbf{Car} ($661$ half-minutes). Much closer: from $10$ to $80$~km/h the middle reading is
within about $5$~km/h of GPS. Only above $80$~km/h does it read low ($71$ where GPS measured
$92$). The average error is $7.5$~km/h.
\item \textbf{Walking} ($43$ straight half-minutes). About $5$~km/h where GPS measured about
$4.5$, just after a ride as at any other time.
\item \textbf{Plane} (one flight). While taxiing the app reads low (about $12$~km/h
where GPS measured $18$). In the air the cabin is smooth, and the speed model reads it as almost
standing still.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Distance}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/fig2_distance.pdf}
\caption{Distance by the app against GPS. (a,\,b)~Each dot is one journey with at least $1$~km that
GPS could check; the shading is within $20\%$. (c)~Straight stretches of walking, added up.
(d)~Distance so far on one flight.}
\label{fig:distance}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($39$ journeys, $177$~km checked). The app added $160$~km, $10\%$ short:
the distance lost at high speed outweighs what is gained at low speed. Of the $38$ journeys with at
least $1$~km checked, the middle one was $6\%$ short and $74\%$ were within $20\%$; the worst were
$63\%$ short and $51\%$ long.
\item \textbf{Car} ($33$ journeys, $145$~km checked). The app added $144$~km, $1\%$ short. Of the
$30$ journeys with at least $1$~km checked, the middle one was $1\%$ short and $63\%$ were within
$20\%$; the worst were $46\%$ short and $61\%$ long.
\item \textbf{Walking}. Over straight stretches the app was $4\%$ long: $7\%$ long in the first
minutes after a ride and $7\%$ short at other times.
\item \textbf{Plane}. The speed model counted $1.8$~km of the $74$~km flown.
\end{itemize}

Motorcycle and car together, the app added $304$~km where GPS measured $322$~km, $5.5\%$ short.

\subsection{Direction}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/fig3_direction.pdf}
\caption{How often the direction is within a given angle of GPS, compared every second with a
half-minute of GPS track (ten seconds for the plane).}
\label{fig:direction}
\end{figure}

Direction holds up in every case (Figure~\ref{fig:direction}):

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($40$ recordings): within $30^\circ$ $72\%$ of the time; middle error
$17^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Car} ($24$ recordings): within $30^\circ$ $69\%$ of the time; middle error $20^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Walking} (seven walks with the phone in a pocket): within $30^\circ$ $93\%$ of the
time; middle error $10^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Plane} (one flight): within $30^\circ$ $99\%$ of the time; middle error $6^\circ$.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:routes} and Table~\ref{tab:summary} put speed and direction together.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Motorcycle} ($40$ routes, $349$~km, many with a walk at one end). The median route is
turned $8^\circ$ and $85\%$ are within $30^\circ$. Routes come out small --- a median
$0.85$ of the real size --- partly because distance is short, and partly because small direction
errors make the path fold. The drawn position drifts from GPS by a median of $17\%$ of the distance travelled.
\item \textbf{Car} ($25$ routes, $126$~km). The median route is turned $20^\circ$ and $56\%$ are
within $30^\circ$; the size is right (median $0.98$). The drift is $18\%$ of the distance travelled.
\item \textbf{Walking}. Only one route was mostly walking, $2.5$~km long; it was turned $53^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Plane}. No route can be drawn, because speed in the air is not measured.
\end{itemize}

Nine routes are turned more than $45^\circ$. Seven of them are short journeys, under $3$~km, with too
few turns to learn the pocket angle well.

\subsection{The flight, second by second}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig5_flight.pdf}
\caption{One flight, from the gate to the climb. (a)~The direction of travel from the GPS track
(black) and from this method, which uses no GPS (purple). (b)~The aircraft's speed from GPS, to show
where it was taxiing, taking off and climbing.}
\label{fig:flight}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:flight} follows the flight with the same direction method as on the road. While
taxiing, the middle error was $6^\circ$; in the air it was $7^\circ$. Nine seconds were more than
$30^\circ$ off. One is the pushback at the start, when the aircraft moves tail first: the method gives
the way the nose points, which is opposite to the way it moves. The other eight came while the
aircraft was stopped or crawling before the runway, where the GPS track has no clear direction. In the climb the gap grows slowly, to about $13^\circ$ by the
end of the recording. Part of that may be wind, which pushes an aircraft sideways so that its nose
and its track differ; GPS alone cannot tell the two apart.

\section{Where it goes wrong}\label{sec:wrong}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{On a motorcycle, fast riding reads slow and slow riding reads fast.} Above
$60$~km/h the speed is under two-thirds of the true value (Figure~\ref{fig:speed}a). Over a whole
journey the two partly cancel, but a journey that is mostly fast comes out short. A car does not
have this problem below $80$~km/h.
\item \textbf{The first minute of a ride.} Until the pocket angle is learned, the direction comes
from the compass alone, which in a pocket can be far off. The redrawing fixes the saved route
afterwards, but a very short ride may never learn the angle well.
\item \textbf{A phone that moves.} The pocket angle is assumed to stay the same for the whole
ride. If the phone shifts, the rest of the ride is turned by about as much as it moved. When the
phone is taken out of the pocket, the hand's movement hides the vehicle's vibration, so the last
speed is held; on one ride, $48$~km/h was held for ten seconds after the motorcycle had stopped,
about $130$~m that was never travelled.
\item \textbf{Car-park ramps.} A car crawling up a concrete ramp shakes like one driving at
$40$~km/h, so distance is not counted while climbing and turning together. The same test can
fire on an ordinary rising, curving road: once for nine seconds at $48$~km/h, about $115$~m lost.
\item \textbf{Aircraft.} Speed cannot be measured in an aircraft. In the air the cabin is smooth,
so a speed model that has learned only from roads reads it as nearly standing still
(Figure~\ref{fig:speed}d): $1.8$ of $74$~km. The app watches cabin pressure and, once it sees a climb,
stops using its road examples and uses only examples learned on earlier flights that had GPS; with
one flight recorded, that could not be tested. The phone's own orientation filter also treats the
long, steady push of a takeoff as a tilt~\cite{quatcf}. The
direction still works (Figure~\ref{fig:flight}).
\end{itemize}

\section{What did not work}

Many ideas were built and tested against GPS before the current version. The most instructive:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Neural networks.} A network that reads $20$--$40$ seconds of fingerprints at once
was more accurate on test data ($8.8$ against $10.6$~km/h) and ran in the app for a while, but
after it was retrained it under-read a pocketed ride by a third, and it was removed.
\item \textbf{Direction from speeding up and braking.} The push when accelerating in a straight
line points forward, so it should give direction. It was $44^\circ$ off; turns work far better.
\item \textbf{Other direction ideas}: the axis the motorcycle leans about ($25^\circ$ off), the
direction of the rider's thigh ($73^\circ$ off), and the swing of the thigh for telling forward
from backward when walking (wrong more often than right).
\item \textbf{How examples were forgotten.} When the store was full, it used to throw away
examples from whichever speed it held most of, to keep rare fast examples. That left it holding
$5\%$ of its examples below $5$~km/h when $24\%$ of real riding is that slow, so slow riding read
far too fast. Throwing away a random example instead fixed most of it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Lessons}\label{sec:lessons}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The phone's own definitions caused more error than the models.} Its built-in heading
becomes unreliable in a common pocket position, and its ``user acceleration'' points the opposite
way to the vehicle's acceleration. Each of these, once found, mattered more than any change to
the estimators.
\item \textbf{A test must not use what it is testing without.} Twice during development the
no-GPS test mode quietly used GPS for direction, which made its results look better than they
were.
\item \textbf{Check what a counter stores while nothing reads it.} The step detector kept counting
while the phone rode in a pocket --- about one bump a second --- and only a walk ever emptied it. The
next walk then paid out hundreds of metres at twice the walking pace. Splitting the results by kind
of travel is what exposed it: walks right after a ride came out twice their length. A ride now
clears the count.
\item \textbf{Check what a learning system remembers.} The store's way of forgetting examples was
sensible on its own and still shifted every answer, and it went unnoticed until its contents were
compared with the riding they came from.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Limits of this evaluation}

All recordings were made by one person, on one iPhone, mostly in one city, by motorcycle, car,
on foot and on only one flight; other people, phones and vehicles may behave differently. Most results come from running the
newest method again over earlier recordings rather than from live use, though on the journeys the
app itself recorded, the re-run came out typically 5\% shorter than the app had drawn. GPS in a pocket is itself only accurate
to tens of metres, so small errors cannot be measured reliably.
