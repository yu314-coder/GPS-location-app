\begin{abstract}\noindent
When GPS is lost --- in a tunnel, an underground car park or an aircraft --- a phone can still draw
the route it travels. This paper describes a system that reads \emph{speed} from how the vehicle
shakes and \emph{direction} from the phone's gyroscope, accelerometer and magnetometer, using GPS for
nothing but the first point, and measures it against GPS on a motorcycle, in a car, on foot and on a
plane. Every recording was replayed through the newest version from its raw sensor data, with GPS
used only as the answer key: $\nJourneys$ motorcycle and car journeys ($\nHours$~hours, $\nKmChecked$~km that GPS could
check), $\nWalkStretches$ straight stretches and $\nWalks$ longer walks on foot, and one flight. Direction was within
$30\dg$ of GPS $\nMotoWthirty\%$ of the time on a motorcycle, $\nCarWthirty\%$ in a car, $\nWalkWthirty\%$ on foot and $\nPlaneWthirty\%$ on the
plane, and the median route was turned $\nMotoRtMed\dg$ on a motorcycle and $\nCarRtMed\dg$ in a car. Against GPS, distance came out
$\nMotoDist\%$ on a motorcycle, $\nCarDist\%$ in a car and $\nWalkDist\%$ on foot. In the air, a network
trained on $302$ airline flights from NASA's public flight recorder data, reading only the time since
the takeoff roll and the phone's tilt, raised the flight's distance from $\nHoldShare\%$ to $\nNetShare\%$, and a store
of examples from the same data to $\nStoreShare\%$; drawn with no GPS after the first point, the flight ended
$\nFlEndNet$~km from where GPS did. A phone with no examples of its own starts with a small
built-in network, which on recordings it had never seen was \nNetVsStore{} a full store; both built-in
networks are drawn layer by layer. We also report what each part of the direction method adds, where the
system fails, and the approaches that did not work.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} dead reckoning; smartphone sensors; GNSS-denied navigation;
vehicle speed estimation; heading estimation; magnetometer
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A phone that loses GPS still knows a great deal about how it moves. Its accelerometer feels every
push and every bump, its gyroscope measures every turn, its magnetometer senses north, and its
barometer feels height. In principle that is enough to keep drawing a route through a tunnel, an
underground car park or a flight. In practice two things make it hard.

The first is \emph{speed}. Adding up acceleration, the textbook answer, does not work on a phone:
its small sensor errors grow into kilometres within minutes~\cite{groves,memserrors}. The second is
\emph{direction}. The gyroscope measures turns but not north; the magnetometer measures north but in
a vehicle it is disturbed by steel, and in any case it gives the direction the \emph{phone} points,
not the direction of travel, which differ by however the phone happens to sit in a pocket or a
holder~\cite{pdrheading}.

This paper describes a system built around those two problems, running as a mode of an ordinary
iPhone app, and measures how well it works. Its contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A complete route recorder that uses GPS for the first point only.} Speed comes from a
fingerprint of the vehicle's vibration, looked up in a store of the phone's own earlier trips or, on
a new phone, answered by a small built-in network. Direction comes from the vehicle's turns, which
reveal how the phone sits, on top of a heading whose slow drift is measured at stops and whose
starting error is corrected by the magnetometer whenever the magnetic field proves to be Earth's, by
holding still in the world while the vehicle turns (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{An evaluation against GPS that reflects the newest version, one kind of travel at a time.}
Every recording was replayed through the current method from its raw sensor data
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}), and each kind of travel has its own section and figures: the motorcycle, walking,
the car and the plane (Sections~\ref{sec:moto}--\ref{sec:flight}). Appendix~\ref{app:journeys} lists every
journey.
\item \textbf{How the built-in networks work.} The two small networks that answer before a phone has
learned anything --- one for speed on the ground, one for the air --- are drawn layer by layer, with how they
were trained and how well they do (Section~\ref{sec:network}).
\item \textbf{An account of what matters.} Every kind of travel side by side, what each part of the
direction method adds (Section~\ref{sec:across}), where the system goes wrong, and the approaches that did
not work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Inertial navigation.} Integrating acceleration twice gives position, but the error grows
quickly --- with the square of time for an accelerometer bias and faster still for a gyroscope
bias~\cite{groves}; smartphone accelerometers and
gyroscopes carry biases and noise large enough to make unaided integration useless within
minutes~\cite{memserrors}. Practical systems therefore constrain the problem.

\paragraph{Pedestrian dead reckoning.} For walking, the usual constraint is the step: count steps,
estimate their length and their direction~\cite{harle}. A sensor on the foot can reset its velocity
at every stance~\cite{foxlin}; a phone in a pocket cannot, and its heading depends on how it is
held, which several methods try to resolve~\cite{pdrheading}. Our walking mode follows the
step-counting tradition; its direction method is our own.

\paragraph{Orientation.} Orientation filters fuse gyroscope, accelerometer and magnetometer
readings~\cite{madgwick}, and on an iPhone Apple's Core Motion provides one. Such filters assume gravity is
the only steady force, so a vehicle's sustained acceleration --- a takeoff roll --- is mistaken for a
tilt~\cite{quatcf}. We found two more practical problems with the built-in heading, in a pocket and
in a car (Section~\ref{sec:direction}).

\paragraph{Vehicle speed from vibration.} Road vibration changes with speed, and learned models can
read speed from accelerometer data alone~\cite{carspeednet}. Our speed model is of that kind, but it
learns continuously from the phone's own trips and must work for a phone in a trouser pocket on a
motorcycle as well as in a car.

A route is a series of short steps, one per second. Each step needs a length and a direction; with
GPS both come from satellites, here both come from the phone (Figure~\ref{fig:system}).

\subsection{Speed}\label{sec:speed-method}

\paragraph{A fingerprint of the vibration.} Every second the phone takes the last five seconds of
up-and-down acceleration, measured $50$ times a second, and reduces it to eleven numbers: the energy
of the shaking in nine frequency ranges and two measures of its overall strength. A vehicle shakes
differently at different speeds, and the fingerprint captures that.

\paragraph{A store of the phone's own trips.} Whenever GPS is available the phone stores fingerprints
with the speed GPS measured at that moment, keeping up to $4{,}000$. Without GPS it looks up the $12$
stored fingerprints closest to the current one and averages their speeds, nearer ones counting more:
\begin{equation}
\hat v = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12} v_j / d_j^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{12} 1 / d_j^2},
\end{equation}
where $v_j$ is a stored speed and $d_j$ the distance between fingerprints. If even the closest stored
fingerprint is far away, or the closest ones disagree among themselves, the store gives no answer
rather than a guess, and the starting network below answers in its place: the speed never waits for
GPS and never stalls while the vehicle moves. Averaging pulls every answer
toward the middle, so a correction learned from the stored examples stretches the answers back out.

\paragraph{A starting network.} A new phone has no examples, so until it holds $3{,}000$ it uses a
small network ($11$ inputs, two layers of $32$, one output) trained on recorded trips and shipped with
the app. It needs nothing from the current trip, and it declines a fingerprint unlike anything it was
trained on.

\paragraph{Stops and hands.} A stopped vehicle is recognised from the absence of shaking, not from
the speed model, which would read an idling engine as a crawl. A hand on the phone shakes it, and
shaking reads as speed, so while the phone is being handled the speed may fall but not rise, and a
phone held in the hand keeps the last speed measured before it was picked up.

\paragraph{In the air.} A smooth cabin reads as standing still. In the air the phone instead measures
the takeoff: from the last moment it was still, it follows its own turning with the gyroscope and
adds up the push the accelerometer feels for up to two minutes. Core Motion's own orientation cannot
be used for this, because it takes a takeoff's steady push for a tilt~\cite{quatcf}. Integration
beyond two minutes gathers too much error, so the phone used to hold the speed it had reached, which
misses the speed-up in the climb.

\paragraph{After the takeoff: two flight engines.} What a phone in a cabin can still sense is how long
ago the takeoff roll began and how it is tilted: an aircraft climbs nose-up and levels off as it
reaches cruise. The phone measures its tilt once a second against the way gravity pointed at the
still moment before the roll, and forms four inputs: minutes since the roll began, the average tilt
over the last minute and the last five minutes, and the tilt added up since the roll began. Two
engines turn these into a speed, in the same pair as on the ground. The \emph{flight network} has four
inputs, two layers of $32$ and one output. The \emph{flight store} holds $4{,}000$ examples and answers
like the phone's store, averaging the $12$ nearest with weights $1/d^2$. Both were trained offline on
NASA's public flight recorder data (Section~\ref{sec:data}), with the recorded ground speed as the
answer. They use no GPS and nothing from the current trip, and inputs beyond the range of the
training flights are held at its edge. The network is used, the store stands in if the network is
missing, and the air examples from the phone's own earlier flights take precedence when there are
any. None of this is used on the ground.

\subsection{Direction}\label{sec:direction}

\paragraph{A heading reference.} The phone's built-in heading follows a direction inside the phone
that depends on how it is held, and when that direction points nearly straight down --- a phone
head-down at an angle in a trouser pocket --- it scattered by about $49\dg$ a minute although the
phone did not move. The system therefore computes its own heading from the phone's orientation,
using whichever edge of the phone lies closest to level; in the same position that heading scattered
by about $1\dg$ a minute.

\paragraph{Drift, measured at stops.} In a car the heading also drifts slowly with the gyroscope's own
bias, because the steel around it keeps the magnetometer from holding north: on one drive it turned
$42\dg$ in $25$~minutes with the phone fixed in place. A stopped car does not turn, so any turning the
heading shows while the car is stopped and the phone still is drift. The phone measures it as a rate,
at most $3\dg$ a minute, and takes the accumulated drift off every heading. It counts a still second only
while the magnetic field reads like Earth's here, $30$ to $60~\mu$T: Core Motion's heading leans on the
magnetometer, and in a disturbed field it is pulled toward the disturbance --- which in a car is not the
same standing as driving, so a drift measured at stops would be carried wrongly into the drive.

\paragraph{The magnetometer, when the field can be trusted.} Core Motion also sets north once, in the
first seconds of a recording, and then follows the gyroscope: on the recordings with the magnetometer
logged its north moved by up to $160\dg$ in the first second and afterwards seldom more than $5\dg$ in a
minute beyond what the gyroscope measured. Set inside a vehicle, that north is often wrong. On one drive
it began $20$--$26\dg$ off while the magnetometer, read directly, put the phone within $1$--$5\dg$ of the
truth. So the phone compares the magnetometer's heading with its own and takes the difference, less the
drift already measured, as the correction to where the heading started --- but only from a field that
has shown it is Earth's. Earth's field is fixed in the world; a field from the car, or from something
carried with the phone, turns with them. Over each last minute in which the heading swept at least
$30\dg$, the phone expresses the field in Core Motion's world frame; if it stayed within $8~\mu$T of its
mean, at a strength of $25$ to $70~\mu$T, that minute was Earth's field and its average difference is a
sample of the correction. Until the first such minute, a field between $25$ and $65~\mu$T whose strength
and dip have been steady for a minute is used instead. Beside a MagSafe charger the phone read up to
$2{,}600~\mu$T and nothing is taken from it.

\paragraph{The angle of the phone, from turns.} The heading says where the phone points, not where the
vehicle goes; the two differ by an angle $\beta$ that depends on how the phone sits. In a turn the
vehicle is pushed toward the inside of the curve, with a push equal to speed times turn rate, so
comparing the sideways push the phone feels with the turning the gyroscope measures shows which way
is forward. Written as complex numbers,
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta = \arg\left(\frac{\sum_{R} z_t}{\sum_{R} |v_t\omega_t|}+\frac{\sum_{L} z_t}{\sum_{L} |v_t\omega_t|}\right),
\qquad z_t=\frac{a_t}{|a_t|}\,\overline{i\,v_t\,\omega_t},
\end{equation}
where $a_t$ is the horizontal acceleration relative to the heading, written as its sideways and forward
parts, $v_t$ the speed, $\omega_t$ the turn rate, and $R$ and $L$ the seconds turning right and left.
Braking into a corner and pulling away out of it lean the answer one way in right turns and the other
way in left ones, so the two kinds are averaged apart. Only seconds above $14$~km/h count, and only
when the turn explains at least a tenth of the push; otherwise the push is braking or throttle, and a
reversing car's push points backwards. The first estimate comes after $30$~seconds of turning, and
whenever the estimate improves the part of the ride already drawn is redrawn with it.

\paragraph{Walking.} Over four seconds the back-and-forth of the steps lines up with the direction of
walking, and each push-off is sharper than each landing, which tells which end of that line is
forward. Steps are counted as peaks of the vertical acceleration and turned into distance with the
stride Apple's pedometer has learned; the walk takes the larger of that and the pedometer's own
total. In some pockets each footfall rings twice, with an echo about a quarter of a second after it,
so the rhythm of the last four seconds is found from the signal's autocorrelation and, while it is
clear, a peak sooner than $0.65$ of its step period is refused as an echo.

\subsection{The route}

After the first point, each second's step is laid from the previous point along the current
direction, for the distance the speed covers in that second. No later GPS position, course or speed
enters the route, the heading or the decisions between walking and riding; the magnetic
declination is taken once, at the first point. Appendix~\ref{app:params} lists every setting (Table~\ref{tab:params}).

\subsection{Recordings}\label{sec:data}

One flight is too few to learn the air speed from, so the two flight engines were trained on NASA's
public DASHlink archive of flight recorder data~\cite{dashlink}: the recordings of one regional jet in
airline service, of which $302$ flights include a takeoff. Each flight was reduced to one value a

is an answer; the inputs are the four the phone forms itself, timed from the start of the takeoff
roll, taken as the last moment under $30$~knots before liftoff.

\subsection{Measuring the newest version}\label{sec:eval}

So that every result describes the newest version, each recording was replayed through the current
method from its raw sensor data:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Speed:} the same fingerprint, the same $12$-neighbour average and correction, refusals
answered by a starting network trained without that recording, and the same rules for stops and a
phone in the hand. The store holds
$4{,}000$ examples drawn at random from every \emph{other} journey, so no journey is measured with its
own GPS in the store. Each example keeps the same chance of being drawn whatever recordings are added,
so a new recording changes the other journeys' stores only by its own examples. On $\nRecN$ recent
journeys the replay differed from what the app itself had drawn by a median $\nRecDiff\%$.
\item \textbf{Direction:} the heading reference, drift correction, magnetometer correction (where the
magnetic field was recorded), the angle learned from turns and the redrawing, with the replayed speed.
\item \textbf{Starting network:} each recording measured by a network trained without it (the
recordings split into eight groups), and the store tested holding only $60$ to $4{,}000$ examples.
\item \textbf{Walking:} the step detector replayed on the recorded acceleration with the stride the
phone had learned.
\item \textbf{The flight:} the app's own code for the takeoff integration, the tilt and both flight
engines, compiled and run on the flight's raw samples; its direction is replayed with that speed in
the air, as the app has it.
\item \textbf{Flight engines on NASA flights:} each flight measured by engines trained without it (the
flights split into five groups), from the start of the roll to landing, against holding the speed
two minutes into the roll.
\end{itemize}
Three inputs are taken from the recordings as they were: whether each second was walking or riding,
Apple's motion classifier, and the car-park ramp flag. Cabin pressure was not recorded, so on the
flight the moment the app would call it airborne was taken from the same integration's climb rate.

In a pocket GPS is itself accurate only to $10$--$40$~m, so it is used over stretches long enough for
that not to matter.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Speed and distance} are compared over half-minute stretches between GPS fixes better than
$40$~m: the straight distance GPS moved against the distance the system added. For walking, only
straight stretches between fixes better than $25$~m are used.
\item \textbf{Direction} is compared every second: the bearing from the GPS position $15$~s earlier to
the one $15$~s later, against the system's heading over the same half-minute ($10$~s for the plane).
\item \textbf{Whole routes} are compared by finding the rotation and the size change that best lay the
drawn route over the GPS track, pinned at the shared start, for routes of at least $1$~km. The
rotation is how far the route is
turned; the size change how much too big or small it is; the drift is how far the drawn end lies from
GPS's, as a share of the distance travelled.
\end{itemize}

$\nCarParks$ car journeys went into, started in or ended in underground car parks. There GPS kept claiming
$10$~m accuracy for positions it did not have --- frozen while parked, wandering on the ramps --- so
those stretches, found from the barometer, are left out of every comparison. So are half-minutes that
mix walking and riding, and those in which the app counted steps while GPS showed riding speed.

\subsection{How the speed network works}

Its inputs are the fingerprint of Section~\ref{sec:speed-method}: the energy of the up-and-down shaking in
nine frequency bands from $0.4$ to $24$~Hz, and two measures of its overall strength --- its spread, and how
sharply it changes from one sample to the next. Each is standardised with a mean and scale fixed from the
training data, never from the phone's own store, so clearing the store cannot change the network. Two
layers of $32$ units with ReLU activations follow, then one output unit with a softplus, which keeps the
answer above zero: $1{,}473$ numbers in all, evaluated once a second.

An averaging model pulls its answers toward the middle, so the output goes through a calibration, as the
store's does: the network's answers on its training data at $21$ quantiles are matched to the true speeds
at the same quantiles, and each new answer is mapped along that line. And like the store it may refuse:
the training fingerprints were grouped into $32$ clusters, and a fingerprint farther from every cluster
centre than $99.5\%$ of the training fingerprints gets no answer rather than a guess.

It was trained on the GPS-labelled fingerprints of the recorded trips, with a Huber loss (which weighs large
errors less than a squared loss does), the Adam optimiser and $40$ passes over the data. The app uses it until
the phone's own store holds $3{,}000$ examples. For the results below each recording is measured by a
network trained without it, the recordings split into eight groups.

\subsection{How the flight network works}

In the air the fingerprint says nothing, since a smooth cabin reads as standing still, so the flight
network's four inputs come from the phone's tilt instead (Section~\ref{sec:speed-method}): minutes since the
takeoff roll began, the average tilt over the last minute and the last five minutes, and the tilt added up
since the roll. Inputs beyond the range of the training flights are held at its edge, then standardised;
two layers of $32$ ReLU units follow, and the single output is the ground speed in hundreds of km/h:
$1{,}249$ numbers. It was trained on every third second of the $302$ NASA flights (Section~\ref{sec:data})
with the Adam optimiser, stopping when a held-back tenth of the data stopped improving. The flight store
beside it holds $4{,}000$ of the same examples and answers with the $12$ nearest. How both do on NASA's
flights and on the recorded one is in Section~\ref{sec:flight}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Where it goes wrong}\label{sec:wrong}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{On a motorcycle, fast riding reads slow and slow riding reads fast.} Above $60$~km/h the
speed is under two-thirds of the true value (Table~\ref{tab:moto}). Over a journey the two partly
cancel, but a journey that is mostly fast comes out short.
\item \textbf{A constant turn over a whole drive.} Much of the remaining direction error is one angle that
lasts the whole drive: $\nTurnBig$ of the $\nTurnN$ graded motorcycle and car recordings are turned by more than
$15\dg$ on average. Removing one such turn per drive --- which needs GPS, so it only bounds what is
possible --- would raise the share of seconds within $30\dg$ from $\nTurnNow\%$ to $\nTurnFixed\%$ and cut the median
error from $\nTurnMedNow\dg$ to $\nTurnMedFixed\dg$. It comes from where the heading started and from the angle learned for
the phone, and the replay cannot separate the two. On the car drive with a clean magnetic field the
magnetometer cut it from $\nMagOffD\dg$ to $\nMagOffE\dg$, and the seconds within $30\dg$ rose from $\nMagWD\%$ to $\nMagWE\%$.
\item \textbf{The first minute of a ride.} Until the angle of the phone is learned the direction comes
from the heading alone (Figures~\ref{fig:moto}d and~\ref{fig:car}d). The redrawing fixes the saved route afterwards,
but a very short ride may never learn the angle well.
\item \textbf{A short, slow drive.} The angle is learned only while the speed model reads more than
$14$~km/h, so on a short drive in traffic it rests on a few turns, and which turns depends on the speed
model. On one car drive, ten minutes at a median $9$~km/h, the speeds the app read on the day kept
$\nSlowAppPct\%$ of the $\nSlowGraded$ graded seconds within $30\dg$ of GPS. Replayed with the speed model rebuilt from the
other journeys, a different handful of turns sets the angle each time that model changes:
\nSlowReplayTxt{} of those seconds were within $30\dg$.
\item \textbf{A phone that moves.} A hand shakes the phone and shaking reads as speed. A hand can no
longer raise the speed, but a phone picked up while slowing keeps the speed it had when it was picked
up. A phone that shifts in the pocket turns the drawn route by about as much as it moved, until enough
new turns have been learned.
\item \textbf{A store full of another vehicle.} The store keeps $4{,}000$ examples and each new one
replaces a random old one, so a run of car drives leaves it mostly car. On the next two motorcycle
rides it declined $\nForgetDeclLo$--$\nForgetDeclHi\%$ of the riding seconds, and the app, which then held its last
answer, read $\nForgetLiveA\%$ and $\nForgetLiveB\%$ of GPS as recorded. The newest version answers those seconds
with the starting network, which on the same seconds reads $\nForgetNetA\%$ and $\nForgetNetB\%$. The re-run draws its
store from every journey, so it cannot show the forgetting itself.
\item \textbf{A disturbed field at stops.} On one $49$-minute drive the field read about $80~\mu$T at stops
and $165$ at speed. The stops then measured a drift that did not happen while moving, and the route turned
further off every minute: with every still second counted, $\nLongDriftEvery\%$ of seconds were within $30\dg$; with no drift
at all, $\nLongDriftNone\%$; counting only stops in Earth's field, $\nLongDriftEarth\%$. The bound matters: allowing up to $65~\mu$T let
the bad stops, at $62$ to $65$, back in. Only the recordings with the magnetometer logged, $\nMagRecs$ so far, can show
this at all, and the gain rests on that one drive.
\item \textbf{A field that looks like Earth's but is not.} On the same drive the steady-field test found
seconds of Earth-like strength, steady for a minute, and took the correction from them; GPS shows it was
about $30\dg$ wrong, and those seconds dipped $14$--$28\dg$ where Earth's field here dips about $35\dg$. A
steady field proves little while a car stands or runs straight. Checked sample by sample against GPS on
the $\nMagRecs$ recordings with the magnetometer logged, the minutes that passed the turn check put north
within $15\dg$ of the truth $\nTurnSampFifteen\%$ of the time (median error $\nTurnSampMed\dg$, worst $\nTurnSampWorst\dg$, $\nTurnSampN$ samples); the
steady-field seconds did so $\nCleanSampFifteen\%$ of the time (median $\nCleanSampMed\dg$, worst $\nCleanSampWorst\dg$, $\nCleanSampN$ samples).
Over those recordings' graded seconds the turn check moved the share within $30\dg$ from $\nMagPooledF\%$ to
$\nMagPooledG\%$. Matching the field's strength and dip to Earth's values instead was fragile
(Table~\ref{tab:failed}).
\item \textbf{A magnet beside the phone.} By a car's MagSafe charger the phone read up to
$2{,}600~\mu$T, fifty times Earth's field, yet reported its compass as well calibrated; the iPhone's own
Compass then drifted $49\dg$ with the gyroscope. The app ignores such a field. On the drive with the
charger beside the phone throughout, a few turning minutes still showed Earth's field,
\nMagSafeTxt{}; a phone that never leaves such a field cannot be
corrected at all.
\item \textbf{Car-park ramps and rides never recognised.} A car crawling up a concrete ramp shakes like
one at $40$~km/h, so distance is withheld while climbing and turning together; once that test fired on
an ordinary road and dropped $9$~seconds at $48$~km/h, about $115$~m. On $\nStepRideN$ journeys
the step counter took the engine for footsteps: as recorded, $\nStepRideGps$~km that GPS measured at
$10$~km/h or more were counted by steps as $\nStepRideLive$~km. On $\nStepRideBig$ of them that was a fifth of the
ride or more, at worst $\nStepRideWorst\%$. Stretches counted by steps while GPS showed riding speed are
left out of the comparisons (Section~\ref{sec:eval}), so this loss is not in the distances reported above.
\item \textbf{In the air, a typical speed.} The flight engines give the speed an airliner of one type
typically has at that point of a flight, not a measurement of this one. A headwind or tailwind, a
faster or slower aircraft, a long hold or a short hop will make them read high or low: on NASA's
flights the middle $80\%$ counted $91$ to $118\%$ of the distance. Examples learned on the phone's own
earlier flights with GPS take over when there are some, which one flight could not test. The
direction in the air settled about $\nFlOffset\dg$ off the track, which carried the drawn end of the flight
$\nFlEndNet$~km from GPS's (Figure~\ref{fig:flightroute}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lessons}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The phone's own definitions caused more error than the models.} Its built-in heading
becomes unreliable in a common pocket position, drifts in a car and can start wrong; its ``user
acceleration'' points the opposite way to the vehicle's acceleration. Each of these, once found,
mattered more than any change to the estimators.
\item \textbf{GPS can hide in decisions as well as in distance.} The no-GPS mode never drew a metre from
GPS, yet at times it used GPS to decide between walking and riding, to set the first heading, or to
carry an angle learned before the mode was switched on. Only reading every place the mode touched GPS
found them.
\item \textbf{Two errors can hide each other.} On one drive a worse angle estimate drew a better route,
because it cancelled an error in the heading reference. Judge a part of the method against physics,
not only by the route it draws.
\item \textbf{A replay must do everything the app does,} stops and a phone in the hand included; with
those rules in, it differs from what the app drew by a median $\nRecDiff\%$.
\item \textbf{A replay must feed each part what the app feeds it.} The flight's direction was at first
replayed with the cabin's near-zero vibration speed rather than the speed the app has in the air. That
starved the angle learner, and the result in the air swung between $4\dg$ and $24\dg$ with nothing but
the replay's random choices; with the app's own speed it holds at $10$--$13\dg$.
\item \textbf{A replay's own randomness can move results.} When a recording was added, drawing every
journey's store afresh moved numbers that had nothing to do with it by several points. Each example now
keeps its own fixed chance of being drawn, so a new recording changes each store only by its own
examples --- which, small as they are, still move single journeys (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

All recordings come from one person and one iPhone, mostly in one city, with a single flight; other
people, phones and vehicles may behave differently, the starting network most of all. The flight
engines learned from one aircraft of one regional jet type and were checked on the phone on a single
flight of another; the phone measures tilt from gravity, which a steady push can bend, while the
recorder measured the aircraft's attitude. Most results replay the newest method on earlier recordings
rather than coming from live use, with a speed store drawn from the same person's other journeys; on a
short drive the replayed and the live speeds can teach different angles (Section~\ref{sec:wrong}).
Single journeys depend on the store's exact contents: adding one eight-minute car drive changed
about $1\%$ of each journey's store and moved single journeys' distance by a median $3.6$ points (up to
$14$), and the motorcycle and car totals by $0.3$ and $1.3$ points, so the shares of journeys within
$20\%$ move by several journeys from one re-run to the next. GPS in a pocket is
accurate only to tens of metres, so small errors cannot be measured reliably. In every journey above
the phone rode in a pocket, lay flat or sat on a mount. On one car drive it was held in the hand the
whole way; recorded live with the current version and kept out of every result, that drive counted
$\nHandDist\%$ of the distance GPS measured and missed the speed by $\nHandMae$~km/h, and above $50$~km/h
it read $\nHandFastApp$~km/h where GPS said $\nHandFastGps$. The rule for a phone in the hand applies only
while it is being moved: the stretches it marked read $\nHandFlagged\%$ of GPS, and those held steadily,
which it did not mark, $\nHandSteady\%$. The magnetic field was
recorded on $\nMagCars$ car drives and $\nMagMotos$ motorcycle rides so far, and everything said here about it rests on
those $\nMagRecs$. On the first drive a MagSafe charger sat near the phone for part of the time and the
correction worked: $\nFirstMagF\%$ of seconds within $30\dg$ with the steady-field test, $\nFirstMagG\%$ with the turn check. On
the second it sat there throughout; the field was steady at an Earth-like strength for only $\nMagSafeClean$
seconds, short of the minute the steady-field test needs, while turning minutes passed the turn check
at $\nMagSafeTurn$ seconds ($\nMagSafeF\% \to \nMagSafeG\%$). On the third, with the phone placed away from the
charger, the field swung between $32$ and $74~\mu$T from minute to minute and was never steady; the turn
check passed at $\nThirdMagTurn$ seconds and the share within $30\dg$ moved from $\nThirdMagF\%$ to $\nThirdMagG\%$. On the fourth
the field rose with speed, from about $70$ to $170~\mu$T, and changed even on straight roads, which is why
subtracting it as a fixed disturbance did not work (Table~\ref{tab:failed}); no minute passed the turn
check, so the steady-field test decides that drive ($\nLongDriveG\%$). On the fifth, $52$ minutes long, the turn check
passed at $\nFifthMagTurn$ seconds, most of them in the first minutes, and put north close to where GPS says it was; but
once those samples were twenty minutes old and dropped, a few scattered later ones moved the correction
the wrong way for the rest of the drive. The share within $30\dg$ stayed high ($\nFifthMagG\%$; $\nFifthMagD\%$ with no
magnetometer at all), because on that drive the angle learned from turns carried an error of the
opposite sign. On the sixth the turn check passed at $\nSixthMagTurn$ seconds, most of them in the first minutes, and
its correction agreed with GPS to within a few degrees, though the drive's share within $30\dg$ was $\nSixthMagG\%$ against $\nSixthMagD\%$
with no magnetometer at all. On the first motorcycle ride the field was
steady for $\nMotoMagClean$ seconds and the correction took the graded seconds within $30\dg$ from $\nMotoMagD\%$ to $\nMotoMagF\%$
($\nMotoMagG\%$ once the turn check, passing at $\nMotoMagTurn$ seconds, takes over), though weak GPS left only $\nMotoMagN$ seconds
to grade; on the second the field stayed near $500~\mu$T, ten times Earth's, nothing could correct the
heading, and $\nMotoTwoG\%$ of its seconds were within $30\dg$. On the third it swung between about $45$ and $150~\mu$T while
riding and passed the turn check only on the walk afterwards, so the ride's direction rested on its
turns alone ($\nMotoThreeG\%$ within $30\dg$). On the fourth it ran from about $40$ to over $1{,}000~\mu$T, was steady for only
$\nMotoFourClean$ seconds and never passed the turn check, and its direction too came from its turns ($\nMotoFourG\%$).

\section{Conclusion}

A phone's own sensors can draw a usable route without satellites. Direction is the stronger half:
learned from the vehicle's turns on a heading kept honest by its stops and, when possible, by the
magnetometer, it stays within $30\dg$ of GPS most of the time on every kind of travel measured. Speed
from vibration is close in a car and on foot but compressed on a motorcycle. In the air, the takeoff
measured by the phone and then a network trained on public airline flight data counted the flight's
distance to within $\nFlWithin\%$, where the takeoff alone counted $\nHoldShare\%$. The largest remaining error is a
constant turn over a whole drive, from where the heading started and from the angle learned for the
phone, which so far only a clean magnetic field has been able to reduce.

\paragraph{Code and data.} The app, including the method described here, is open source at
\url{https://github.com/yu314-coder/GPS-location-app}. The recordings contain the author's locations and
are not published; the airline flight data is public~\cite{dashlink}, and the two flight engines ship
with the app.
