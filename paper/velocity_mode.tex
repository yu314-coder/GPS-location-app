\begin{abstract}\noindent
A phone can record where it has been without satellite positioning, reading speed from how the
vehicle shakes and direction from its motion sensors. We describe the method as shipped in a
consumer iOS workout application and measure it against GPS recorded alongside, used only as a
reference, over journeys by car, motorcycle and on foot. Driving fast and steadily with the phone
resting in the vehicle, distance is within $3.8\%$ over $15$~km; in slow stop-start traffic the
same system first read $20$--$60\%$ long. Most of that gap was a caching policy rather than the
signal: the store kept rare fast examples by evicting from its most crowded speed bin, which left
it holding $5\%$ of its examples below $5$~km/h where the riding produced $24\%$. Replacing a random
example instead, and calibrating by percentiles, left later rides roughly unbiased on average,
though single rides still vary by tens of percent. Direction needs no satellite at all: the angle at
which the phone sits is learned from the sideways acceleration of the vehicle's turns, and walking
is steered by the axis of the steps. Compared route by route with GPS over $60$ recordings and
$451$~km, the whole route is rotated by a median $13^\circ$ and within $30^\circ$ on $88\%$ of
recordings, against $31^\circ$ and $48\%$ for the phone's compass alone and $73\%$ for earlier
builds that learned the angle from GPS. With rotation and size removed all three draw the same
shape: the gyroscope gets the shape right, and what separates a good route from a bad one is how
far the whole of it is turned and how long it is. We report in full over twenty approaches that
were implemented, measured and rejected --- among them a sequence network that beat the store
offline and was withdrawn after it failed in the field --- and the platform semantics that cost
more than any modelling choice: a vendor sensor fusion that removes the signal aircraft dead
reckoning needs, a platform heading that goes singular in a pocket, and an acceleration reported
with the opposite sign to the vehicle's.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Satellite positioning fails where routes are most often wanted: tunnels, underground car parks,
dense urban canyons, and aircraft cabins. The standard response is inertial dead reckoning ---
integrate acceleration twice --- which is well known to diverge, since accelerometer bias is
indistinguishable from sustained real acceleration and its double integral grows without bound.

An alternative exists for wheeled vehicles. Road and engine vibration scale with speed, so a
spectral signature of vertical acceleration can be mapped to speed directly, without
integration and therefore without accumulation. CarSpeedNet~\cite{carspeednet} reports
RMSE $1.8$~m/s and MAE $0.72$~m/s from $13.2$~hours of accelerometer-only driving, at the same
$4$-second window length we arrive at independently. Its $0.5$-hour test holdout is drawn from
that same collection, so the reported figure describes journeys much like the ones it learned from.

This paper describes such a system built into a shipping application, and --- more usefully ---
reports what happens outside the distribution, on a phone that is sometimes mounted, sometimes
pocketed, sometimes in a hand, in a car, on a motorcycle, and once in an aircraft.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item An out-of-distribution evaluation of vibration-derived speed across carry positions and
vehicle types, on one device and one user, with per-regime error.
\item A cache-policy bias: an eviction rule that protects rare fast examples silently reshapes
the distribution the estimator answers from, accounts for most of the low-speed over-read, and is
fixed by changing the eviction, not by reweighting at lookup (\S\ref{sec:cache}).
\item Direction without GPS --- a heading that cannot go singular, the carry angle from cornering,
walking direction from the step axis --- and a route-level comparison with GPS over $60$
recordings that separates each route's error into rotation, size and shape
(\S\ref{sec:direction}, \S\ref{sec:routes}, Appendix~\ref{app:routes}).
\item The measured cost of the specific-force ambiguity on a consumer platform: two approaches
defeated, and a third we had blamed on it withdrawn.
\item Negative results in full, including a sequence network that beat the store offline and was
withdrawn in the field, and two approaches that look correct in aggregate and fail per query.
\item Platform and systems defects that silently invalidated data --- among them a test mode that
used the GPS it was meant to exclude, and four meanings of ``heading'' --- offered as
evidence that platform semantics dominate modelling error in this setting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Speed from vibration.} The closest work is CarSpeedNet~\cite{carspeednet}, a CNN
taking three-axis smartphone accelerometer input, reporting RMSE $1.8$~m/s and MAE $0.72$~m/s
over $13.2$~hours of driving. Related approaches estimate speed from chassis
vibration with CNNs~\cite{cnnspeed}, infer position directly from vehicle vibration with an
IMU~\cite{vibposition}, and use learned momentary speed to constrain strapdown inertial
navigation~\cite{constrainsins}. Outside the inertial domain, vehicle speed has been estimated
from tyre--road noise, where band energies at $1$--$4$~kHz give $1.4\%$ average
error~\cite{soundspeed} and the underlying physics --- tread-block passage at
$f = NV/L$ --- gives a direct speed dependence.

Our estimator is deliberately simpler than these: a $k$-NN regressor over log-spectral band
energies, chosen because it can \emph{decline} to answer when nothing resembling the query has
been observed, which a trained network cannot. The contribution here is not the estimator but
the evaluation regime.

\paragraph{Published accuracy is usually measured on familiar data.} CarSpeedNet's $0.5$-hour holdout comes
from the same $13.2$-hour collection as its training data. This matters: we measure $+1.0$ to $+1.7$~km/h bias on
a store built from matched journeys and $+6.8$~km/h on the same device once the store contains
other carry positions and vehicles --- a degradation invisible to a random split. The
transportation-mode literature has confronted this more directly; the Sussex--Huawei Locomotion
dataset~\cite{shl} was assembled specifically to enable reproducible cross-user evaluation,
with benchmark F1 near $0.93$ for eight-class mode recognition~\cite{shlbench}. No equivalent
public dataset exists for \emph{speed} regression across carry positions.

\paragraph{Inertial dead reckoning.} That double integration of consumer MEMS output is
unusable is well established: laboratory characterisation of smartphone IMUs reports position
error exceeding $1$~km within $100$~s from pure inertial
propagation~\cite{memserrors}, with error growth dominated by uncompensated bias and varying
substantially between handsets. Learned approaches to inertial odometry~\cite{solin} and the
broader survey of deep inertial positioning~\cite{dlsurvey} attack this by replacing integration
with regression, as we do. Standard mitigations --- zero-velocity updates~\cite{zupt} and map
matching~\cite{mapmatch} --- bound rather than remove the drift.

\paragraph{Heading and carry position.} Pedestrian dead reckoning divides into step detection,
heading estimation and position propagation~\cite{pdr3d}, and heading is consistently the
limiting term. Carry position is known to dominate: measured heading standard deviations of
$6.22^\circ$, $6.82^\circ$ and $14.68^\circ$ for holding, calling and swinging respectively,
with corresponding final position errors of $2.11$, $2.34$ and $4.5$~m~\cite{pdrheading}. Our
$\beta$ term is the vehicular analogue of the carry-mode misalignment that literature addresses
for pedestrians. An earlier draft of this paper reported that $\beta$ cannot be recovered without
an external reference. That was wrong: a vehicle's turns reveal it (\S\ref{sec:direction}).

\paragraph{The specific-force ambiguity.} An accelerometer measures specific force and cannot
separate gravity from sustained linear acceleration. This is standard, and attitude-estimation
work explicitly compensates for it, either by estimating disturbance acceleration
adaptively~\cite{disturbaccel} or by weighting the accelerometer correction by how far its
magnitude departs from $g$~\cite{quatcf}. What we add is the downstream consequence on a
platform where the fusion is not ours to modify: the vendor-supplied attitude and
gravity-removed acceleration are the only interface, and their handling of sustained
acceleration silently defeats two otherwise reasonable designs (\S\ref{sec:onecause}).

\section{Method}

\subsection{Signature}

Vertical acceleration $x[n]$ is sampled at $50$~Hz into a ring buffer of $N = 256$ samples
($4.0$~s), windowed and transformed:
\begin{align}
w[n] &= \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)\\
X[k] &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\big(x[n]-\bar{x}\big)\,w[n]\,e^{-2\pi i k n / N}
\end{align}
Energy is accumulated into nine logarithmically spaced bands bounded at
$0.4$, $0.8$, $1.6$, $2.5$, $4$, $6$, $9$, $13$, $18$ and $24$~Hz:
\begin{equation}
f_b = \ln\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k \in b} |X[k]|^2 + \epsilon\Big), \quad b = 1\ldots 9
\end{equation}
Two time-domain terms complete an eleven-dimensional signature:
\begin{equation}
f_{10} = \ln \sigma(x), \qquad
f_{11} = \ln\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{N-1}\textstyle\sum_n |x[n]-x[n-1]|\Big)
\end{equation}

The window length was measured rather than assumed. At $4$~s the held-out bias is
$+1.0$~km/h at $2$--$10$~km/h; doubling to $8$~s raises it to $+6.0$, because the window
then straddles changes in speed. Finer low-frequency banding changed nothing.

\subsection{Speed}

No functional form is imposed. The system stores $(\mathbf{f}, v)$ pairs whenever GPS supplies a
speed, and answers by locality. With running feature means $\mu_i$ and variances $\sigma_i^2$,
\begin{align}
d^2(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{11}\left(\frac{f_i-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}-\frac{g_i-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^{\!2}\\
\hat{v} &= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K} w_j v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} w_j},
\qquad w_j = \frac{1}{d_j^2 + \epsilon}
\end{align}
with $K = 12$, where $\pi_j$ corrects for how over- or under-represented a neighbour's speed is in
the store relative to how often that speed was actually travelled:
\begin{equation}
\pi(v) = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{ridden}}(\text{bin}(v))}{P_{\text{store}}(\text{bin}(v))},\;0.05,\;20\right),
\qquad w_j = \frac{\pi(v_j)}{d_j^2 + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
$P_{\text{ridden}}$ is a decaying histogram of every observation as it was learned, over $2$~km/h
bins, with a half-life of about $1400$ observations; $P_{\text{store}}$ is the same histogram of
what the store currently holds. The two differ because of the eviction policy, not because of
anything about the signal, and \S\ref{sec:cache} shows that difference accounts for most of the
low-speed over-read. Both are $1$ on a fresh install, where the estimator reduces to the
unweighted form above. Crucially the estimator is allowed to decline:
\begin{equation}
\min_j d_j^2 > 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{no answer}
\end{equation}
Error scales directly with match distance --- MAE $5.3$~km/h below $d^2\!=\!1.03$, rising to
$16.9$ in the range $2.12$--$4.36$ --- so refusing a distant match removes the worst readings.

The store holds $4000$ observations. Once it is full, an incoming observation replaces one chosen
at random, with two exceptions: the fastest $2\%$ of ground speeds are never chosen, so the store
keeps evidence for speeds it rarely sees, and a ground observation never replaces an airborne one.
\S\ref{sec:cache} explains why this replaced an earlier rule, and why $\pi$ remains only as a small
correction while an old store turns over.

Averaging twelve neighbours pulls every answer toward the middle of their speeds. A monotone
calibration undoes that: over held-out stored observations, the $q$-th percentile of what the
estimator predicts is mapped onto the $q$-th percentile of what GPS measured, at $21$ points, and
every answer is passed through that map. Finally the \emph{displayed} speed is smoothed over
$2$~s; distance is integrated from the unsmoothed estimate, because smoothing lags a real change
and costs a few percent of distance.

\subsection{Regime separation}

A store holding several vehicles holds several mutually incompatible mappings. Measured
directly: a ground-trained model reported $19$~km/h at a $900$~km/h cruise, because cruise is
\emph{quieter} than taxiing and the slowest stored signature was the closest match. After flight
data entered the same store, a city drive reported a mean of $337$~km/h against a true $30$.
Air and ground are therefore stored and searched as separate partitions.

\subsection{Direction}\label{sec:dirmethod}

Turn angle comes from the gyroscope, absolute direction from the magnetometer:
\begin{equation}
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} + \Delta\psi_{\text{gyro}}
+ \kappa\big(\psi_{\text{datum}} + \beta - \theta_{t-1}\big), \qquad \kappa = 0.25
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the \emph{carry offset}: the angle between where the phone points and where the
body or vehicle travels. Earlier versions learned $\beta$ from GPS course during the first
$180$~s of a workout and then froze it. The current one uses no satellite information after the
first point, in four parts.

\paragraph{A datum that cannot go singular.} $\psi_{\text{datum}}$ is not the platform's heading.
Core Motion reports the bearing of a direction fixed in the phone, and which direction depends on
how the phone is held; when it points near vertical the reading is noise (\S\ref{sec:headings}).
Instead, with the attitude $R = R_z(\text{yaw})\,R_x(\text{pitch})\,R_y(\text{roll})$ mapping the
device frame to north--west--up, we take the bearing of whichever device axis lies most nearly
horizontal --- the one least aligned with gravity, switching only when another is flatter by $0.15$
in $|\cos|$ --- and carry the difference between the two axes across a switch so the reading stays
continuous. Only offsets are ever learned against it, so its absolute zero does not matter.

\paragraph{The carry offset from cornering.} In a turn a vehicle accelerates toward the inside of
the turn. Writing the horizontal acceleration in the datum's frame as a complex number
$a = a_{\text{fwd}} + i\,a_{\text{right}}$, with $v$ the estimated speed and $\omega$ the gyroscope
turn rate (clockwise positive),
\begin{equation}
a_t \approx e^{i\beta}\,(i\,v_t\,\omega_t)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\hat\beta = \arg \sum_t \frac{a_t}{|a_t|}\;\overline{i\,v_t\,\omega_t}
\end{equation}
over every riding second at $v \geq 4$~m/s. Normalising $a_t$ keeps one pothole from outvoting a
minute of corners. The acceleration must be the physical one, which is the \emph{negative} of what
Core Motion reports as user acceleration (\S\ref{sec:headings}). The first estimate is made after
$30$ riding seconds.

\paragraph{Walking from the steps.} With the phone in a pocket the compass was $56$--$137^\circ$
from the direction of walking on five of the seven walks graded, so walking is steered by the steps
instead. Over
$4$~s, the principal axis of horizontal physical acceleration is the line of travel, and its third
moment along that axis is positive toward the front, because push-off is sharper than heel-strike.
Each window's vote enters a circular median of (vote $-\ \psi_{\text{datum}}$) over the last $20$
windows, which picks the end of the current window's axis; the heading is pulled toward it at
$0.5$ per second. A window counts only if the phone's posture held within $15^\circ$ across it,
and settling at a new angle ($25^\circ$) starts the record afresh. A phone lying flat
($|g_z| > 0.7$, in the hand) takes the end nearer the datum instead, because the vote came out
reversed on the one flat walk recorded.

\paragraph{Redrawing.} Every drawn point keeps the heading and offset it was drawn with. When a
ride's $\hat\beta$ moves by $3^\circ$, every point of the current ride is turned by the difference;
once a walk has eight counted windows, its opening windows are redrawn with the settled median. The
saved route therefore carries the best estimate reached, while the live display carries the one
available at the time. A ride begins at $2.5$~m/s, and at that moment the heading is set to
$\psi_{\text{datum}} + \hat\beta$ outright, because sitting down turns the phone about a horizontal
axis the gyroscope's vertical rate does not see.

\subsection{Projection}

\begin{equation}
\Delta s = \hat{v}\,\Delta t, \quad
\phi_t = \phi_{t-1} + \frac{\Delta s \cos\theta_t}{R}, \quad
\lambda_t = \lambda_{t-1} + \frac{\Delta s \sin\theta_t}{R\cos\phi_t}
\end{equation}
Only the first point of a route originates from GPS.

\section{Results}

as the estimate, and \emph{estimated} means what the app produced from the motion sensors alone.

$15$~km journey those two differ by $35\%$ ($14{,}445$~m against $19{,}496$~m), because position
scatter adds length that was never travelled.

\subsection{Why slow journeys read long}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_bias.pdf}
\caption{How much the estimate reads above the real speed, by real speed. Tested by hiding each
stored example in turn and asking the model to recover it.}
\label{fig:bias}
\end{figure}

The ordering in Table~\ref{tab:journeys} follows average speed, not any property of the route, and
Figure~\ref{fig:bias} explains why. Tested against $4{,}178$ stored examples from matched journeys
--- hiding each one in turn and asking the model to recover it --- the estimate reads
$1.0$ to $1.7$~km/h high at \emph{every} speed.

A fixed error in km/h is not a fixed error in percent. Two km/h on a $50$~km/h road is $2\%$; the
same two km/h on a $5$~km/h crawl is $40\%$. So the estimator that looks excellent on a motorway
looks poor in traffic, without anything about it having changed.

This is also why no correction helps. Subtracting a constant $6$~km/h --- fitted on two journeys
where the estimate happened to read exactly that much high --- repaired those two and made three
others worse, reducing total absolute error across all journeys by only $7\%$.

That account is correct as far as it goes, and it is not the whole story. It was measured by
holding out stored examples, so it describes the error on the distribution the store holds. The
riding has a different distribution, and \S\ref{sec:cache} shows that the difference between the
two --- created entirely by the cache's eviction rule --- is the larger term.

\subsection{The cache policy is the bias}\label{sec:cache}

The store holds $4000$ observations. When it is full, an incoming observation displaces one drawn
from the most crowded $2$~km/h speed bin. The intent is to stop a rare $100$~km/h example being
squeezed out by thousands of red lights, and it achieves that. What it also does is make the store
stop resembling the riding.

A query that lands between regimes is answered by whatever its twelve nearest neighbours happen to
be, and after rebalancing most of them are fast. That predicts a specific signature of error:
correct where the store is dense in both versions, wrong and high where the rebalancing has thinned
the evidence. This is what the shipped application does --- within a few percent above $50$~km/h,
roughly double at $15$--$30$~km/h.

To test it rather than assert it, the estimator was reimplemented offline against the same feature
extraction, the same $K$, the same decline threshold, and trained leave-one-ride-out on the other
rides' labelled windows. The only variable is how the training pool is assembled.

Two things follow from Table~\ref{tab:cache}. The estimator was never the problem: on a pool that
was never rebalanced it is within $8\%$ on the same rides, using the same eleven features that
\S\ref{sec:pocket} had concluded carried no speed. And the fix does not require giving up the
eviction rule --- weighting each neighbour by $\pi(v)$ recovers the same $8\%$ while keeping the
rare fast examples an aircraft partition depends on. The residual gap between the replay's $20\%$
and the shipped $36\%$ is the store's older content, which the replay cannot reconstruct.

We record this as a modelling lesson of the same kind as the platform defects in \S6: nothing about
the eviction rule is wrong locally, it is sound cache management, and it was invisible for weeks
because the store's contents were never compared against the distribution they were drawn from.

\paragraph{What shipped instead.} The weighting of Table~\ref{tab:cache} did not survive contact
with the field. The ridden histogram is a decayed count of the last few rides, while the store was
built over months, and the ratio between the two is not the correction the store needs: the next
build's logs showed a median weight of $1.01$ at $15$--$30$~km/h where about $4$ was needed, and
replayed as shipped the weighting gave $22\%$ against $22\%$ without it. What converges is changing
the eviction itself. Replayed from a store built under the crowded-bin rule through seven rides in
order, that rule stayed at $5\%$ slow observations throughout and the last four rides averaged
$82\%$ distance error; replacing a random observation instead reached $11\%$ slow and $13\%$ error
over the same four rides, improving from the very next one. That is what ships (\S3.2), with the
weighting kept as a residual correction worth $13\% \to 11\%$ in the same replay.

After it and the percentile calibration, whole-ride distance over eight later rides was roughly
unbiased, $-1\%$ to $-4\%$ pooled. What remains is compression of the speed range: $5$--$15$~km/h
still reads about double and $50$--$80$~km/h about a quarter low, and mean absolute speed error
stays near $9.7$~km/h whatever correction is applied --- the floor set by the signature, not by
the estimator. Figure~\ref{fig:similarity}(d) shows the same history in the recorded routes: drawn
at a median $1.02$ times their real size in August, $1.27$ in the first half of September, and
$0.98$ in the last week.

\subsection{Direction: the angle the phone sits at}\label{sec:direction}

The phone rarely points the way the vehicle travels. It sits at some angle in a cradle, a pocket
or a cup holder, and that angle --- $\beta$ in \S\ref{sec:dirmethod} --- has to be subtracted before
the recorded heading means anything.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_heading.pdf}
\caption{Why the angle matters, measured with the earlier GPS-learned version. The same journeys
with the angle unlearned and learned. Left: how far the whole route is rotated. Right: how far the
finish point lands from where it really was.}
\label{fig:heading}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:heading} shows what that is worth. With the angle unlearned the route comes out
the right shape and the right length and simply points the wrong way: on the $15$~km journey the
recorded straight-line displacement matched the real one to within $8$~m while being rotated
$26^\circ$, which put the finish $4{,}174$~m from where it actually was. Learning the angle moves
the finish to $366$~m, $2.4\%$ of the distance travelled. Those figures come from the earlier
version, which measured the angle from GPS in the first three minutes and then held it.

\paragraph{Without GPS.} The current version learns the angle from turns and uses no satellite
information after the first point (\S\ref{sec:dirmethod}). To see what that costs, the direction
logic was replayed through every recording that logged the phone's attitude: $61$ recordings from
$15$~August to $24$~September, car and motorcycle, mounted and pocketed, $476$ minutes of riding
over $344$~km. Direction is replayed; distance is left as each build recorded it, so the routes
below carry whatever speed error was current at the time. GPS enters only as the grader: for every
half-minute of riding, the bearing between the GPS fixes $15$~s either side --- at least $150$~m
and four times their accuracy apart, each better than $60$~m --- against the circular mean of the
drawn heading over the same half-minute.

\subsection{Routes as a whole}\label{sec:routes}

A heading error per half-minute does not say what the finished route looks like. So each recording's
drawn route was compared with its GPS track as a whole, three ways: the phone's compass alone, the
route as recorded at the time, and this method --- all three with the distance the phone recorded,
so they differ only in direction. For every recording the drawn and GPS positions at the same
instants were paired, and the rotation and scale about the shared start that best lay one on the
other were found by least squares:
\begin{equation}
c = \frac{\sum_i \bar z_i\, w_i}{\sum_i |z_i|^2}, \qquad
\text{rotation} = -\arg c, \qquad \text{size} = 1/|c|, \qquad
\text{shape} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i |c\,z_i - w_i|^2}{\sum_i |w_i|^2}}
\end{equation}
with $z_i$ and $w_i$ the drawn and GPS positions as complex numbers east~$+\,i\,$north. Rotation is
the direction error of the route as a whole, size is the distance error, and shape is whatever is
left: $0$ is the same route turned and scaled. GPS fixes are those under two seconds old and better
than $40$~m, with impossible jumps removed; $60$ recordings qualify, $451$~km of GPS track.

We report these in full because each cost real effort and two share one cause.

\subsection{One cause, three failures}\label{sec:onecause}

\textbf{Core Motion's attitude filter reinterprets sustained linear acceleration as a change in
the gravity direction.} The ambiguity itself is standard~\cite{disturbaccel,quatcf}; what is
reported here is its measured cost when the fusion is vendor-supplied and cannot be replaced. A takeoff roll is the most sustained acceleration a phone will ever
experience, and is therefore precisely what the filter removes. This defeated two designs, and
we had wrongly blamed it for a third:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \emph{Aircraft speed integration}, above.
\item \emph{Deriving $\beta$ from acceleration} --- withdrawn. A vehicle accelerates along its
axis, so the world-frame acceleration bearing should give the travel direction; measured on three
drives, circular concentration was $R = 0.15$, $0.18$, $0.19$, and we attributed that to the
fusion. Most of it was ours. Core Motion's user acceleration is the reaction the phone feels, the
negative of the vehicle's acceleration, so every bearing was reversed; and the frame was built by
guessing between the attitude matrix and its transpose, which a phone lying flat cannot tell apart.
Corrected, straight-line speed changes still give a poor answer --- $44^\circ$ median error ---
but the sideways acceleration of turns gives the carry angle to about $11^\circ$
(\S\ref{sec:direction}).
\item \emph{Inertial takeoff detection.} Integrating positive along-track acceleration and
latching at $45$~m/s in one unbroken episode should identify a takeoff and nothing else.
Replayed: today's car $52.5$~m/s (latched), a second car $209.9$ (latched), a motorcycle $328.5$
(latched), a two-minute \emph{walk} $119.7$ (latched), and the actual flight $15.4$ --- never
latched. Backwards on every case. The episode rarely breaks in a vibrating vehicle because road
noise holds the signal above the floor, so the integral accumulates the positive half of the
noise indefinitely.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Regime identification does not transfer}

Whole-session fingerprints --- the mean normalised signature of a workout --- separate regimes
cleanly. Across eight sessions, same-vehicle-and-carry pairs sit $0.54$--$1.70$ apart,
same-vehicle-different-carry $1.81$--$2.87$, and different-vehicle $4.30$--$6.97$.

Restricting the neighbour search by that fingerprint nevertheless made prediction \emph{worse}
($+2.2 \rightarrow +3.4$~km/h). The ranking is unreliable at the individual level: for one car
journey the nearest stored session was a car ($1.38$), but the second was a \emph{hand-held}
session ($1.54$) and the third a \emph{motorcycle in a pocket} ($1.74$), both ahead of the same
car driven the same morning ($2.29$).

\subsection{On a motorcycle in a pocket the signature carries no speed at all}
\label{sec:pocket}

Every failure above is a matter of degree: the estimate is too high, or too low, or right about
the wrong regime. This one is different in kind. Over a $19.1$-minute motorcycle ride with the
phone in a trouser pocket --- $1146$ ticks at a clean $1$~Hz, Velocity Mode on for $1143$ of
them, three GPS fixes in the drawn route --- the reported speed and the real speed are
statistically unrelated: $R = +0.13$. The vibration signature and the real speed are related at
$R = -0.02$, which is to say not at all.

signalled that it was answering about a regime it had never seen.

\subsection{Correction: the pocket signature does carry speed}\label{sec:pocketfix}

\S\ref{sec:pocket} is the strongest claim in this paper and it is partly wrong. The quantity
measured there at $R = -0.02$ against real speed is a scalar from the superseded hand-built
estimator --- a scaled dominant vibration frequency, which the diagnostics file still records
beside the learned model's output --- and not the eleven-dimensional signature \S3.1 defines.
The two were conflated. Re-measured on four motorcycle rides with the phone in a trouser pocket
under open sky, the signal is present and strong:

was no longer the one being used. An instrument that reports a superseded quantity beside a
current one, with no indication of which is which, produces confident wrong conclusions in the
analyst as readily as in the application.

\subsection{Confidence signals that do not work}

Neighbour disagreement is the natural confidence measure for a $k$-NN regressor. It fails here
in the wrong direction: out-of-distribution motorcycle queries had a \emph{tighter} neighbour
spread than in-distribution car queries ($5.2$ vs $12.4$~km/h), because they land consistently
in one wrong region. Session-level fingerprint distance correlated with speed bias at
$r = -0.01$ over six journeys.

\subsection{Discriminating a hijacked pedometer}

On a motorcycle, engine vibration is counted as footsteps. Apple's activity classifier reported
\texttt{automotive} on $636$ ticks and \texttt{walking} on $271$ of one continuous ride, and each
flicker allowed the pedometer to take over: it drove $27\%$ of the ride, reporting a mean
$3.3$~km/h while GPS recorded $49.6$.

Three per-tick discriminators were measured against real walking and rejected: vibration energy
($79\%$ caught at $21\%$ false), gait-band spectral concentration (distributions overlap), and
both combined with rotation rate ($69\%$ at $7\%$). What worked was history rather than
instantaneous features: across two real walks the classifier \emph{never} reported automotive in
$190$ stepping ticks, while on motorcycles the median gap since it last did was $84$~s. Requiring
two minutes of silence before a pedometer-driven revocation suppressed $61\%$ of false
hand-overs at zero measured cost.

\subsection{Four more that were measured and rejected}

\textbf{Integrating between stops.} Each detected stop is a known zero, so acceleration along the
travel direction can be integrated between two of them with the drift pinned out at both ends.
Measured over the segments of one ride: $-65\%$ of the real distance using the application's
heading, $-50\%$ using the raw motion heading, and $-75\%$ even when the GPS course was supplied
as a perfect direction. Residual drift reaches $10$--$21$~m/s per segment for a pocketed phone.

\textbf{Time since the last stop.} If the over-read were a pull-away artefact it would concentrate
after a stop. It does not: on two rides the excess distance splits $+64\%$, $+54\%$, $+48\%$ and
$+81\%$ across $10$--$30$, $30$--$60$, $60$--$120$ and $120$~s${+}$ since the last stop.

\textbf{Zeroing an idling engine.} Shake does not separate a stationary motorcycle with its engine
running from one crawling: accelerometer magnitude has median $0.22$~m/s$^2$ stopped against
$0.65$ at $4$--$12$~km/h, heavily overlapped. The best threshold tested removes $1142$~m of
phantom distance across $70$ recordings while zeroing $312$ crawling and $200$ slow-riding ticks
--- about $2000$~m that was really travelled.

\textbf{Reweighting the amplitude features.} Since $f_{10}$ and $f_{11}$ carry the speed
information (\S\ref{sec:pocketfix}), weighting them above the nine band energies in the distance
metric should help. It changes nothing measurable ($8\%$ mean distance error at $\times1$,
$\times2$ and $\times3$). The neighbour search was never the problem; the pool was.

\subsection{A neural network: better offline, withdrawn in the field}\label{sec:neural}

The $k$-NN store is simple on purpose (\S2), so the obvious question is whether a trained network
does better. Seven designs were trained on the same eleven features and scored on held-out rides,
each given the same percentile calibration and the same gates, with mean absolute speed error in
km/h:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item A per-window CNN in place of the store: worse, $11.1$ against $9.4$ on the ground and $88$
against $34$ in the air.
\item The average of the two: worse than the better one alone.
\item A CNN given the store's calibration and gates: closer, never ahead.
\item Spectrum input, augmentation and ground pretraining: the best per-window network, and still
behind.
\item A residual network on top of the store, initialised as an exact copy of it: $11.0$ against
$10.8$, no gain.
\item \emph{Distillation}: a student trained on the store's own answers matches it exactly,
$10.9$ against $10.9$, and needs no GPS labels at all --- the store's function compresses into
$40{,}000$ parameters.
\item A \emph{sequence} model, a GRU over $20$--$40$~s of features: $8.8$ against $10.6$ for the
store, and $9.2$ for the store with its $2$~s display smoothing. Doubling context, layers, data or
seeds bought $0.1$~km/h. Time, not capacity, is what the store lacks.
\end{enumerate}

The sequence model was then shipped for eleven builds, running beside the store on every tick with
a developer setting choosing which one drew the route and both answers logged. On two car drives it
did what the offline numbers promised --- $2.8$ against $3.2$~km/h on $570$ paired ticks --- and
something they had not measured: the store had no answer on $32\%$ of one drive, because a car is
far from a store built on a motorcycle, while the network answered $95\%$ of the time. Its cost
was not a reason to hesitate either: $1.9$~ms and $0.31$~J for a whole ride against $1.1$~ms and
$0.10$~J for the store. A recurrent layer cannot run on the Neural Engine; a convolutional version
that can was $4.7$~km/h against the GRU's $3.6$ on the same split, to save $0.2$~J.

It was withdrawn after a retrain. The retrained network read a pocketed motorcycle ride $34\%$
low where its predecessor had read $13\%$ low, plausibly because its calibration had been fitted on
a training set by then dominated by $8{,}493$ windows of car-mounted motorway driving. The next
ride its owner judged a complete failure, and every build since has run the store alone. The cause
of that ride is not known; its log was never sent for analysis.

A larger lesson came from the same work, on a different network. A small CNN on the raw $50$~Hz
window beat the store's per-tick error on ten of eleven held-out rides, $7.9$ against $14.1$~km/h,
and left distance error by speed band exactly where it was, because the compression comes from
averaging under uncertainty, not from the features. Out of distribution it failed unsafely: on a
flight it had never seen it averaged $223$~km/h of error and recorded $5\%$ of the distance, where
the store declines to answer. A network that is better on familiar data and confidently wrong on
unfamiliar data is the wrong trade for a system whose purpose is the unfamiliar case.

\subsection{Six ways of finding direction that did not work}

Each was built, replayed against GPS on the logged rides, and dropped. Numbers are median direction
error unless stated.

\textbf{The axis the motorcycle leans about.} A leaning bike rolls about its own direction of
travel, so the principal axis of horizontal rotation should give that direction up to a half-turn.
It gives $25^\circ$ even with the half-turn forgiven.

\textbf{The thigh as a pointer.} Walking, gravity in the phone's frame runs down the thigh; seated,
the thigh points forward, so the same device direction should point along the road. It does not:
seated, that direction stayed $54$--$86^\circ$ from horizontal, and the estimate was $73^\circ$ off.

\textbf{A lean-proof forward vector.} Leaning is a rotation about the forward direction, so solving
for that direction in the phone's own frame --- least squares on
$\mathbf{a}_{\text{dev}} = -v\omega\,[\mathbf{u}]_\times \mathbf{f}$ --- should be immune to lean.
It scored $19.9^\circ$ against $15.9^\circ$ for the planar method over $29$ rides, better on six and
worse on nine. Lean is not what limits the estimate.

\textbf{Gating the compass by the gyroscope.} Following the gyroscope and letting the compass move
the baseline only while the two agree within $30^\circ$ improved seven rides by more than $10^\circ$
and worsened five; riding within $30^\circ$ fell from $69\%$ to $65\%$. It was treating a symptom whose cause was the heading
definition (\S\ref{sec:headings}).

\textbf{Switching headings by conditioning.} Using the platform's heading while its reference
direction is well off vertical and the axis heading otherwise made the ride it was designed for
worse, $72^\circ$ to $78^\circ$: every hand-over copies the noisier source into the offset.

\textbf{The thigh's swing, for walking forward or back.} A thigh swings forward faster than it
returns, so rotation about the axis across the direction of travel should be skewed toward the
front. It was wrong in $56$--$62\%$ of two- to six-second windows, because the largest horizontal rotation
of a pocketed phone while walking is about the direction of travel, not across it.

\section{Systems failures}

The first three defects below were not algorithmic, and each silently invalidated data for weeks.

\textbf{A stale reading is not a missing reading.} \texttt{CLLocationManager} suspends heading
updates outside the foreground, and the cached property is never cleared. On an $81$-minute drive
the heading datum held \emph{one value for $4790$ of $4832$ seconds} while the device physically
rotated $5264^\circ$. A fallback to Core Motion's heading had been added for the missing case and
was never reached, because it was written as \texttt{clHeading ?? motionHeading} and a frozen
value is not \texttt{nil}.

\textbf{Background permission gating.} \texttt{allowsBackgroundLocationUpdates} was enabled only
under \emph{Always} authorisation. Under \emph{When In Use} the process was suspended
mid-journey: a single $371$-second hole in a $21$-minute drive, during which the $50$~Hz stream
delivered $49$ samples where $18{,}550$ were due, accounting for the entire $20\%$ distance
shortfall.

\textbf{Instrumentation that cannot express the failure.} A $17$-minute workout the user believed
was recording without GPS had the mode active for $34$~seconds; $63$ GPS fixes entered the route.
The guarantee held --- the code path returns early --- but nothing in the log stated the mode's
state, and it took an hour to establish from a side effect. Two columns now record it directly.

\textbf{A repeated reading is not a new one.} The diagnostics file carries the latest GPS speed,
accuracy and position on every tick, holding the last fix until another arrives --- in Velocity
Mode, up to $45$~s. One ride held a $0$~km/h reading for $33$ consecutive ticks while the rider
pulled away, which scored as the estimator reporting $60$~km/h at a standstill and put that ride at
$+47\%$ where fresh fixes alone give $+36\%$. The column was not wrong; it was undated. Every
figure in \S\ref{sec:cache} is computed against fixes under two seconds old, using an age column
added afterwards.

\textbf{Four meanings of ``heading''.}\label{sec:headings} The direction work was held back for
weeks by platform semantics rather than by the estimator. Core Motion's heading is the bearing of a
direction fixed in the phone, chosen by how the phone is held --- fitted as
$(-0.05, 0.87, 0.49)$ in the device frame on some rides and $(-0.02, 0.46, 0.89)$ on others ---
and when that direction points near vertical the reading is noise. On one ride the phone sat
head-down at $45^\circ$, the direction was within $20^\circ$ of vertical, and over each minute the
heading scattered $49^\circ$ around what the gyroscope measured; the route was drawn $72^\circ$
off. The attitude itself was fine: the most nearly flat device axis scattered $1^\circ$ over the
same minutes. \texttt{CLHeading} follows yet another direction, $25$--$100^\circ$ from Core Motion's
on a tilted phone, and the application handed over between the two whenever it entered or left the
foreground; seven rides whose carry angle had looked like zero turned out to have been measured
against \texttt{CLHeading}. Core Motion's user acceleration is the reaction the phone
feels, the negative of the vehicle's acceleration: on pocketed rides it pointed $139$--$180^\circ$
away from the acceleration GPS measured. And a $30$~s running mean of the vertical rotation rate,
subtracted as gyroscope bias, is a high-pass filter that hands back every turn over the following
minute --- Core Motion's rotation rate is already bias-corrected. The compass correction hides that
last one, and removing it changed nothing measurable, so it remains.

\textbf{A gate that cannot recover.} The estimator refuses to answer when the workout's fingerprint
sits further than $3.0$ from every learned session (\S3.3). That fingerprint is the mean of the
workout's own GPS-labelled observations, and it only grows while GPS supplies a usable speed. On
one ride GPS did so twenty times, during stops and a pull-away, and then effectively stopped: the
fingerprint froze at $6.12$ --- a different-vehicle value, for the same motorcycle that had scored
$0.29$--$1.88$ that morning --- and the gate refused every tick for $9.5$~minutes, recording $20$~m
of a $4.3$~km ride. Across $21$ sessions the fingerprint exceeds $3$ only while a session holds
about twenty observations ($3.35$, $4.47$, $4.78$, each settling to $0.7$--$1.1$) and never passes
$2.57$ after sixty. A refusal now requires sixty. The defect is not the threshold but that a
quantity computed from an eight-sample minimum was allowed to be authoritative and could not be
revised once the evidence feeding it stopped.

\textbf{A test mode that used GPS.} Velocity Mode can be forced on with GPS still available, to
measure what it would do without. Twice that test quietly used GPS for direction. First, the carry
angle was corrected continuously from GPS course --- on $716$ of $720$ ticks of one drive --- and
later, after that was removed, it was learned from GPS during a three-minute warm-up. Every heading
figure from those builds was flattered, and nothing in the output said so. The warm-up was removed
in favour of the cornering estimate (\S\ref{sec:dirmethod}), and the log now records the
learned-without-GPS angle in its own column.

\textbf{A warm-up on the wall clock.} That warm-up was three minutes from pressing Start, not three
minutes of movement, so a rider who warmed the engine and put a helmet on spent it standing still.
The split across the forced rides was total: with no moving seconds inside the window the angle
came out $-147^\circ$, $-33^\circ$ and $+130^\circ$; with $46$--$131$ moving seconds, $-1^\circ$,
$-3^\circ$ and $+2^\circ$.

\textbf{A walking angle applied to a ride.} The angle is learned separately walking and riding,
because a pocketed phone sits differently in each. A correction that rotates the opening stretch
of a route once the angle is known did not check which kind of angle it had: on five of $30$ rides
the first to settle was learned walking away from the bike, and it rotated the whole ride by
$+146^\circ$, $+161^\circ$, $+127^\circ$, $+73^\circ$ and $-55^\circ$.

\textbf{A placeholder averaged as a measurement.} Estimated points carry a nominal $250$~m accuracy
meaning ``dead reckoned''. The workout's average accuracy included them, so $6\%$ of estimated
points turned a real $12$~m average into $26$~m and a quality score of $16/100$ --- which we took,
for a while, as the receiver having degraded. The best-instrumented drive, $2127$ real fixes, has a
median of $14.2$~m and $97\%$ within $20$~m. The receiver was fine.

\textbf{One point, two kilometres.} A single dead-reckoned point once landed $2052$~m from the GPS fix
beside it in time, adding $5.4$~km to a $49$~km recording, while the other $131$ sat a median $79$~m
from the truth. Nothing bounded a step by what the vehicle could have covered since the last one.

Both of the last two were fixed, and both fixes were reverted along with the network
(\S\ref{sec:neural}) when the application was returned to its earlier build wholesale; neither is
in the current version. We report them because a revert of that kind is itself a systems hazard:
it restored the estimator that was wanted and silently removed two unrelated corrections with it.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Hand carry destroys the signal; pocket carry degrades it.} At matched speeds a
hand-held phone reads $0.6$--$0.8$ lower in the superseded summary feature, which stops varying
with speed altogether. A pocket is different and was misdiagnosed for three weeks
(\S\ref{sec:pocketfix}): the signature there does track speed, and a pocketed ride places within
$8$--$10\%$ once the store's composition is corrected for.
\item \textbf{Stationary vehicles with running engines.} A motorcycle at a red light reads
$5.2$~km/h, accumulating $690$~m over $339$ stationary ticks. No tested signal separates an
idling engine from a slow crawl.
\item \textbf{Car-park ramps.} A vehicle crawling a concrete spiral shakes as hard as one at
$40$~km/h on a road; measured error is $+169\%$ to $+297\%$. Detection requires two signals
jointly --- barometric climb above $0.08$~m/s \emph{and} signed net rotation above $150^\circ$
over $30$~s --- since the barometer alone flags every hill and rotation alone every junction.
\item \textbf{Straight ramps are not detected, and it matters less than expected.} The turn
requirement excludes any ramp that does not spiral. The first straight one recorded --- $12.6$~m
of climb over $1.15$~km at $45$--$74$~km/h, under $45^\circ$ of net turning --- showed why the
rule should not simply be relaxed: the estimate there was already right ($1135$~m against
$1153$~m by GPS), so withholding it would have discarded a kilometre of correct distance. A
turn-free climb test tightened to $6$~m of rise at $R^2 \geq 0.9$ still fires on $2169$ ticks
across $64$ recordings, almost all ordinary hills, and on $131$ ticks of a flight's cabin
pressure. Only a \emph{slow} straight ramp needs catching, and slow is the one thing the estimate
cannot vouch for without GPS.
\item \textbf{Unseen vehicles.} The model answers only for conditions it has observed.
\item \textbf{Speed held through a stop.} When the phone is taken out of a pocket the vibration
is the hand's, so the last speed is held rather than measured. Stopping and reaching for the phone
therefore keeps the route moving: on one ride $48$~km/h was held for ten seconds after the
motorcycle had stopped, about $130$~m that was never travelled.
\item \textbf{The ramp detector at speed.} A rising, turning road can look like a car-park ramp. On
one ride the detector withheld nine seconds at $48$~km/h on an ordinary road, about $115$~m.
\item \textbf{Walking pace.} Step cadence times a learned stride read $4$--$7$~km/h on one walk
where GPS measured $3.4$--$4.7$.
\item \textbf{A phone that moves in the pocket.} The carry angle is learned per ride and assumed to
hold. Where the phone slowly rolled in a pocket during a ride the error grew to $30^\circ$, and
redrawing a ride with a later, worse estimate spread it back over the whole ride. A walk that
begins while the phone is still being put away is drawn from the compass until the phone settles.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{One device, one user, three vehicles.} The $23$ journeys of \S4.1 and the $60$ recordings
of \S\ref{sec:routes} were all made on a single iPhone by one person, in one city, using one car,
one motorcycle and walking. Every quantity here
should be read as characterising that configuration. The transportation-mode literature moved to
multi-user public datasets~\cite{shl} precisely because single-subject results do not transfer,
and we have no evidence ours do.

\paragraph{GPS as ground truth.} Errors are measured against GPS recorded simultaneously. Where
GPS is degraded --- underground, and in the urban canyon immediately after --- the reference is
itself uncertain. For walking it can be worse than uncertain: on one $4.5$-minute walk the Doppler
speed averaged $1.06$~m/s while position chords gave $1.6$--$2.2$~m/s depending on the sampling
interval, at $14$--$20$~m accuracy. The accelerometer settles it without GPS --- autocorrelation
gives a $1.05$~s stride period, so the application's $1.55$~m/s implies a $0.85$~m stride and the
Doppler figure implies $0.55$~m, which is not the gait that was recorded. Walking figures in
earlier drafts that rested on GPS alone should be treated as unproven. We use the integral of GPS speed rather than the sum of fix-to-fix
displacements, because the latter accumulates position scatter: on one $15$~km drive the two
differ by $35\%$ ($14{,}445$~m against $19{,}496$~m), and the fix-summed figure is the wrong one.
Segments with no valid GPS speed are excluded and reported separately, but a systematic bias in
GPS speed at low speed would propagate into our low-speed figures undetected.

\paragraph{GPS as the direction reference.} Direction is graded on half-minute chords between
fixes that are often $30$--$60$~m accurate, which puts a floor under every figure: every variant of
the direction logic we replayed scored between $18^\circ$ and $21^\circ$ median error over all
riding, however different its failures. Recordings worse than $40^\circ$, where the reference
cannot be the explanation, are the more telling measure. Walking truth needs a straight half-minute
of good fixes and exists for only seven walks.

\paragraph{No baseline comparison.} We do not evaluate a published method on this data, so we
cannot say whether a CNN trained on the same journeys would degrade the same way outside its
distribution. The comparison we do make --- matched store versus mixed store on identical
held-out data --- isolates store composition, not architecture.

\paragraph{Thresholds fitted on few journeys.} The ramp detector ($0.08$~m/s climb with
$150^\circ$ signed rotation over $30$~s) was selected by sweeping against seven journeys and
choosing the lowest total absolute error. That is a small sample, and one journey regressed from
$-7.1\%$ to $-31.7\%$ under the chosen setting because its ramp over-read had been cancelling an
under-read elsewhere. We report it rather than tuning it away.

\paragraph{Data availability.} The logs are per-tick CSV plus $50$~Hz raw traces, and the results
here are reproducible from them. They are not currently public. Releasing them would make this a
labelled multi-vehicle, multi-carry vibration corpus, which to our knowledge does not otherwise
exist; we consider that the most useful next step.

\section{Conclusion}

Vibration-derived speed is a sound basis for GPS-free route recording within its regime: fast,
steady travel with the phone mechanically coupled to the vehicle, where we measure $3.8\%$
distance error over $15$~km. Outside that regime the failures are not gradual --- a hand on the
phone removes the signal, and an aircraft's defining acceleration is erased by the platform's own
sensor fusion. Direction, by contrast, no longer needs a satellite reference at all. Learned from
the vehicle's own turns and from the steps when walking, it puts whole routes within $30^\circ$ of
their real orientation on $88\%$ of $60$ recordings, ahead of the builds that learned the angle
from GPS, and $93\%$ of walking within $30^\circ$ against $30\%$ for the compass alone. The shape
of a route was never the problem; its rotation was, and its size still is.

We would emphasise four lessons over any result. First, the dominant error sources were not
modelling choices but platform semantics: a suspended process, a stale cached heading, a heading
that goes singular when the phone sits head-down, an acceleration with the opposite sign to the
vehicle's, an attitude filter making a defensible choice that destroys the signal of interest.
Second, a component can be locally correct and globally biasing: the cache's eviction rule is sound
cache management, and it silently reshaped the distribution the estimator answers from
(\S\ref{sec:cache}); where a system learns continuously and stores what it learns, the storage
policy is part of the model. Third, a model that is better on familiar data and confidently wrong
on unfamiliar data is the wrong trade when the unfamiliar case is the purpose (\S\ref{sec:neural}).
Fourth, a test is only a test if it cannot quietly use the thing it is testing without: twice the
GPS-free mode used GPS for direction, and every figure from those builds was flattered.
